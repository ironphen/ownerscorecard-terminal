# Graham and the owner — verified fact base

*2026-07-18. Companion to `docs/organized-owners.md` (the mission memo). Adversarially verified
(workflow `wf_b9e154c6-df6`, 3 lanes, primary sources read directly where stated). Verbatim
accuracy is doctrine: quotes below are tiered VERIFIED / LIKELY-BUT-UNCONFIRMED, and anything
published must cite the edition named here. Local verified text extracts are noted where they
exist in the session scratchpad; re-extract from the named sources for future use.*

The mission Ryan ratified — re-aligning managements with shareholders through the literacy and
organization of the individual **enterprising investor** (Graham's own category) — is not adjacent
to the house lens. It is Graham's own project, stated by him repeatedly across forty years, and
described by him, at the end, as unfinished. This document is the proof, quote by quote.

---

## 1. Forbes, 1932: the mission in Graham's words

"Inflated Treasuries and Deflated Stockholders" (subtitle on the printed page: *"Are Corporations
Milking Their Owners?"*), Forbes, June 1, 1932 — **verified against photographic scans of the
original pages**:

> "EVIDENTLY stockholders have forgotten more than to look at balance sheets. They have forgotten
> also that they are owners of a business and not merely owners of a quotation on the stock
> ticker. It is time, and high time, that the millions of American shareholders turned their eyes
> from the daily market reports long enough to give some attention to the enterprises themselves
> of which they are the proprietors, and which exist for their benefit and at their pleasure."

> "These are not management problems; these are ownership problems."

> "What stockholders need to-day is not alone to become 'balance sheet conscious,' but more than
> that, to become 'ownership conscious.'"

Third article ("Should Rich but Losing Corporations Be Liquidated?", July 1, 1932, scan verified):

> "The stockholders do not have it in their power to make a business profitable, but they do have
> it in their power to liquidate it."

> "If you owned a grocery store that was doing badly, you wouldn't leave it to the paid manager to
> decide whether to keep it going or to shut up shop."

Scan-vs-transcription catches: the original reads "in no uncertain tones" (circulating retypes say
"terms") and "give away their interest" (retypes: "interests"). Prefer the scans.

## 2. Security Analysis, 1934/1940: the docile animal — and the Berle–Means link

Chapter XLIV, "Implications of Liquidating Value. Stockholder-Management Relationships."
1934 first edition pp. 508–509 (verified via facsimile snippets); 1940 second edition p. 594
(read from the archive.org scan):

> "The choice of a common stock is a single act; its ownership is a continuing process. Certainly
> there is just as much reason to exercise care and judgment in being as in becoming a
> stockholder."

> "It is a notorious fact, however, that the typical American stockholder is the most docile and
> apathetic animal in captivity. He does what the board of directors tell him to do and rarely
> thinks of asserting his individual rights as owner of the business and employer of its paid
> officers. The result is that the effective control of many, perhaps most, large American
> corporations is exercised not by those who together own a majority of the stock but by a small
> group known as 'the management.'" *(1940 wording)*

**Edition variance (cite the edition you quote):** 1934 reads ", and never thinks of asserting";
1940 softens to " and rarely thinks of asserting". The which/that in "businesses which/that they
own" is unresolved at the letter level (both readings are OCR-derived).

**The Berle–Means link is Graham's own:** the passage continues directly (p. 509, 1934): "This
situation has been effectively described by Berle and Means in their significant work *The Modern
Corporation and Private Property*." Graham tied his shareholder-docility complaint to the founding
diagnosis of the separation of ownership and control **in the same paragraph, in 1934**.

## 3. The lectures, 1946: "not enough of them are disgruntled"

"Current Problems in Security Analysis," New York Institute of Finance, Sept 1946–Feb 1947 —
**verbatim from the official transcripts hosted by Columbia Business School** (Lecture 8):

> "Consequently, by insensible stages of reasoning, the specialist in undervalued securities finds
> himself turning into that abomination of Wall Street known as a disgruntled stockholder.
> I want to say a word about disgruntled stockholders. The trouble with stockholders, in my humble
> opinion, is that not enough of them are disgruntled. And one of the great troubles with Wall
> Street is that it cannot distinguish between a mere troublemaker or 'strike-suitor' in
> corporation affairs and a stockholder with a legitimate complaint which deserves attention from
> his management and from his fellow stockholders."

Negative finding: no "docile", "sheep", or "apathetic" language appears anywhere in the ten
lecture transcripts (all ten grepped).

## 4. The Intelligent Investor, 1973 (4th ed.): the mission, declared unfinished

Chapter 19, "Shareholders and Managements: Dividend Policy," Graham's text at p. 487 (read from
the Zweig-edition scan):

> "Ever since 1934 we have argued in our writings for a more intelligent and energetic attitude by
> shareholders toward their managements. We have asked them to take a generous attitude toward
> those who are demonstrably doing a good job. We have asked them also to demand clear and
> satisfying explanations when the results appear to be worse than they should be, and to support
> movements to improve or remove clearly unproductive managements."

> "In the last 36 years practically nothing has actually been accomplished through intelligent
> action by the great body of shareholders. A sensible crusader—if there are any such—would take
> this as a sign that he has been wasting his time..."

> "It can be stated as a rule with very few exceptions that poor managements are not changed by
> action of the 'public stockholders,' but only by the assertion of control by an individual or
> compact group." *(pp. 488–489)*

And the closing plea (p. 489): shareholders should "consider with an open mind and with careful
attention any proxy material sent them by fellow-shareholders who want to remedy an obviously
unsatisfactory management situation in the company."

Note: Graham cut the long "Investor as Business Owner" treatment from the 1973 edition (Zweig:
pared "nearly 34 pages" to "less than eight terse pages about dividends"). The sheep language is
not in the 1973 text.

## 5. Northern Pipe Line, 1926–28: Graham's own campaign

**Campaign narrative** verified from Kahn & Milne, *Benjamin Graham: The Father of Financial
Analysis* (Financial Analysts Research Foundation, 1977, pp. 12–15 — Irving Kahn was Graham's
assistant; the closest firsthand account in print). **Numbers** verified from Security Analysis
(1940 text, chs. 41 and 43), which carries the figures but never tells the campaign story.

- Discovery, 1926: reading an ICC annual report for railroad data, Graham noticed pipeline
  statistics "taken from their annual reports to the Commission," obtained the 50-page ICC report
  form, and "took the train to Washington the next day." The eight ex-Standard pipelines published
  one-line income accounts; at the ICC he found them holding investment-grade railroad bonds often
  exceeding their own market value.
- Northern Pipe Line: selling at 65 while holding (per Kahn-Milne) "$95 per share of cash assets,
  mostly in good railroad bonds." Security Analysis's published table (1926): price 64;
  cash-asset value $79/share; current-asset value $82; book value $116. (The $95-vs-$79 gap
  between the two sources is unreconciled — use Security Analysis's figures in print; they are
  Graham's own published record.)
- Graham bought 2,000 of 40,000 shares (5%) through the Benjamin Graham Joint Account — largest
  holder after the Rockefeller Foundation's 23%.
- Management's brush-off, as rendered by Kahn-Milne: *"The pipeline business is a complex and
  specialized business about which you know very little; but in which we have spent a lifetime.
  We know better than you what is best for the company and the stockholders. If you don't approve
  of our policies, you should sell your shares."*
- January 1927 annual meeting (Oil City, Pennsylvania): Graham attended alone; **his motion died
  for want of a second.**
- 1927–28: bought more, retained counsel, solicited proxies by letter, and "Ben and his associates
  visited the larger shareholders." (**"Door-to-door" is later dramatization** — the verified form
  is visits to larger holders plus mail.) Pennsylvania's mandatory cumulative voting meant
  one-sixth of shares elects one of five directors.
- January 1928 meeting: Graham arrived "supplied with proxies for 38 percent of the shares."
  Management proposed a slate including any two rebels *except* Graham; refused; the agreed slate
  seated Graham and one of his lawyers — "the first person not directly affiliated with the
  Standard Oil system to be elected a director of one of the affiliates."
- Weeks later, the president: "We really were never opposed to your idea of returning capital to
  the stockholders; we merely felt the time wasn't appropriate." **$70 per share was distributed**
  (Security Analysis: "$50 per share... in 1928, as a return of capital... Later a second
  distribution of $20 per share"); the Rockefeller Foundation had quietly indicated on its proxy
  that it favored distributing "as much capital as the business could spare." The other
  ex-Standard pipelines followed ("Similar liberal distributions were made by most of the
  pipe-line companies of the so-called Standard Oil group" — Security Analysis, verbatim).

Caveat: quotes attributed to Graham's *Memoirs* (1996) circulate consistently but were not
independently verified against the printed Memoirs (access-gated); treat all Memoirs wording as
secondhand until checked against the book.

## 6. Berle & Means, 1932: the diagnosis

*The Modern Corporation and Private Property* — verified against the Macmillan 1933 reissue scan,
page-anchored:

> "The separation of ownership from control produces a condition where the interests of owner and
> of ultimate manager may, and often do, diverge, and where many of the checks which formerly
> operated to limit the use of power disappear." *(p. 6)*

> "...a large body of security holders has been created who exercise virtually no control over the
> wealth which they or their predecessors in interest have contributed to the enterprise." *(p. 5)*

> "The property owner who invests in a modern corporation so far surrenders his wealth to those in
> control of the corporation that he has exchanged the position of independent owner for one in
> which he may become merely recipient of the wages of capital." *(p. 3; scans OCR "modern" as
> "modem" — the word is modern)*

> "...the owners of passive property, by surrendering control and responsibility over the active
> property, have surrendered the right that the corporation should be operated in their sole
> interest..." *(p. 355, cross-verified against a second independent source)*

The governance literature's shorthand for all of it: the "Berle-Means corporation" — dispersed
passive owners, self-perpetuating management — later formalized as agency costs (Jensen &
Meckling 1976).

## 7. Buffett: the partnership attitude

"An Owner's Manual" (June 1996; current text extracted directly from berkshirehathaway.com/ownman.pdf),
Principle 1, verbatim:

> "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership. Charlie Munger and I think of our
> shareholders as owner-partners, and of ourselves as managing partners. (Because of the size of
> our shareholdings we are also, for better or worse, controlling partners.) We do not view the
> company itself as the ultimate owner of our business assets but instead view the company as a
> conduit through which our shareholders own the assets."

Commentary, same principle: "We hope you instead visualize yourself as a part owner of a business
that you expect to stay with indefinitely, much as you might if you owned a farm or apartment
house in partnership with members of your family."

Principle 2: "In line with Berkshire's owner-orientation, most of our directors have a significant
portion of their net worth invested in the company. We eat our own cooking."

Provenance: the 13 principles were first set down in the **1983 annual letter** (wording differs
slightly — 1983: "we also are"; manual: "we are also"; cite accordingly; the site's HTML drops
apostrophes — flag when quoting). The 1979 letter carries the reporting-duty frame, verbatim:

> "Your Chairman has a firm belief that owners are entitled to hear directly from the CEO as to
> what is going on and how he evaluates the business, currently and prospectively. You would
> demand that in a private company; you should expect no less in a public company."

> "In large part, companies obtain the shareholder constituency that they seek and deserve."

**Negative finding (doctrine-grade):** no annual letter 1977–1997 contains "picture yourself as
part owner." The remembered line is a paraphrase; the canonical wording is the Owner's Manual's
"visualize yourself as a part owner" (1996).

## The through-line

1932: owners, not "owners of a quotation" — become *ownership conscious*. 1934: the docile animal,
tied by Graham himself to Berle–Means in the same paragraph. 1946: "not enough of them are
disgruntled." 1926–28: he did it himself — proxies, a board seat, $70/share returned. 1973, at the
end: "ever since 1934 we have argued... practically nothing has actually been accomplished."

Graham stated the mission, practiced it once, and pronounced it unfinished. He lacked the tools:
records were sealed in ICC filing cabinets, fellow owners were unreachable except by train, and
the watching couldn't scale. Every one of those constraints is now gone. That is the lineage claim
OwnerScorecard's mission stands on — and every quote above is citation-grade for the day it gets
written for publication.
