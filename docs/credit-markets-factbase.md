## 1. The AI bond wave, quantified

- IG AI-related issuance reached **$218B through July 8, 2026** vs **$80.5B in all of 2025**
  (PitchBook LCD, verbatim) — ~2.7× run-rate acceleration within LCD's US-IG scope (~1.6× on
  BofA's broader all-currency measure); **~$250B including high-yield** ($31.9B HY) reconciles
  the WSJ's quarter-trillion.
- **Meta is the single-name case study in scale and structure**: ~$29B of corporate debt at
  the start of Oct 2025 → **~$57B raised in Oct–Nov 2025**: the **$27.3B off-balance-sheet
  "Beignet" JV with Blue Owl** (S&P A+, 6.581%, funding the ~2GW Hyperion campus; confirmed in
  Meta's own 424B2: long-term debt $28,834M before / $58,834M after — the $27B sits OFF the
  balance sheet) plus a **$30B on-balance-sheet deal, tied fifth-largest IG offering ever, on a
  record $125B order book**.
- The pipeline behind future issuance grew all year: consensus 2026 hyperscaler capex escalated
  **$314B → $458B → $518B** during 2025 (Janus Henderson/Goldman), now **approaching $600B for
  2026 and ~$4T cumulative through 2030** (CreditSights/MUFG).

## 2. The Graham test — the text, corrected, and the numbers

- **Fidelity correction to the owner's recollection**: Graham's passage is in **Chapter 6**
  (1972/1973 4th ed., pp. 134–137), and it is framed as **second-grade vs. high-grade bonds,
  not high-grade corporates vs. governments**. Verbatim: the "businessman's investment" is a
  fallacy — *"It is bad business to accept an acknowledged possibility of a loss of principal
  in exchange for a mere 1 or 2% of additional yearly income"* — and *"Since in late-1971 it is
  possible to find first-rate corporate bonds to yield 7¼%... it would not make much sense to
  buy second-grade issues merely for the higher return they offer."* (Ch. 4's closest analog is
  US savings bonds vs corporates for the defensive investor.) The FRAMEWORK transfers exactly:
  an insufficient premium per unit of credit risk is bad business, wherever the rung.
- **The test quantified today, on the risk-taking margin**: Bloomberg US HY OAS closed Nov 2025
  at **2.7% vs a 4.9% twenty-year average**; ICE BofA US HY OAS was still **2.70% on July 9,
  2026** (FRED BAMLH0A0HYM2). Schwab's hit-rate history (daily, 2005–2025): from starting
  spreads ≤3%, high yield beat Treasuries over the next 12 months **only 39% of the time**
  (83% when spreads started ≥5%). This is precisely Graham's "mere 1 or 2%" condition.
- **The IG denominator**: US IG OAS hit **74bp by mid-Sept 2025 — the narrowest since ~1998**
  (ICE BofA record low: 53bp, Oct 1997) — and has stayed in a **~71–80bp** band through July
  2026 (fresh three-decade low ~71bp in late Jan). The index prices essentially no stress.

## 3. Where the supply is actually biting (the marginal repricing)

- Index-wide IG spreads widened only **~10bp** off the September tights despite the mega-deals
  — the absorption case.
- But **10-yr BBB technology spreads went from 12bp TIGHTER than non-financial corporates
  (mid-Sept 2025) to flat in seven weeks** — the tech premium fully erased by supply.
- **Meta's 6.30% 2056s hit their widest yet at T+145 in early July 2026** — 13bp wide of their
  April pricing, 25bp off their tights of a month earlier; the WSJ chart shows Meta's 2036
  spread breaking 1.0pt over Treasurys against the ~0.72 IG average.
- The verified tension in one sentence: **the index is becalmed at multi-decade tights while
  the marginal price of the strongest issuers' new debt rises** — both "the market disagrees
  with the shock thesis" and "the first dial is already moving" are true simultaneously.

## 4. Private credit — idiosyncratic at the headline, understated in the plumbing

- The **FSB (May 6, 2026, 50-page report, read in full)** judged the First Brands and Tricolor
  market reaction *"contained and short-lived"* and attributed the failures to **fraud, opacity,
  hidden leverage, off-balance-sheet financing** — not a cycle turn. Precision matters: the FSB
  calls the *reaction* contained, not the defaults benign — and the same report catalogs the
  channels: weak underwriting "spanned across corporate credit markets," banks lending at three
  levels, insurers writing credit insurance on the defaulted firms, creditors in 11
  jurisdictions.
- **Measurement gap**: defaults read **~1% outright but ~5% including selective defaults**
  (rescue restructurings) — comparable to US high yield; **PIK is present in ~12% of
  private-credit loans** (toggles ~half), rising since 2022, and PIK-toggle use associates with
  a **+1–2pp next-quarter delinquency probability** (Rintamäki & Steffen 2025).

## 5. Software — where AI disruption and private credit intersect (BIS, March 2026)

- Private-credit lending to SaaS firms grew **~$8B (2015) → $500B+ (end-2025) — 19% of ALL
  direct lending**.
- Software equities fell **~30% Oct 2025–Feb 2026**, attributed by the BIS to AI-disruption
  fears; **software-heavy BDCs underperformed low-exposure BDCs by ~5pp** (BDC stocks −10%
  average) — market-priced transmission of the AI shock into private-credit vehicles through
  software loan books, specifically.
- **The maturity wall**: ~**$80B of software debt matures in 2026; ~$386B in 2028–29** (S&P
  universe; ~$162B private-credit-only). Counter-case on the record: Lincoln International's
  tech co-head argues the 2021–22 software buyouts remain "fundamentally good credit," damage
  concentrated in equity values (Lincoln Q1 2026: software enterprise values −8.8% vs loan fair
  values −1.9% of par). PitchBook Q2 2026: private-credit funds declining new software
  borrowers — the strain is in refinancing appetite.

## 6. The demand side

- The indigestion mechanism is institutionally flagged **as a conditional, not a base case**:
  Schwab (Dec 2025, reaffirmed June 2026): tech supply "poses a risk to corporate bond prices
  if demand doesn't keep up" — while the same outlook calls IG attractive (yields near 15-year
  highs, up-in-quality). Record order books (Meta's $125B) argue demand depth; the erased tech
  premium argues the market has begun charging for it.

## 7. The both-ways read (what each thesis REQUIRES)

**For the shock thesis to be right**: the tech-bucket repricing must broaden into index-level
widening; the software maturity wall must meet the already-visible refinancing reluctance and
produce realized losses; the private-credit measurement gap (~1% vs ~5%) must resolve toward
the broader measure; and demand fatigue must show up in new-issue concessions, not just
single-name curves.

**For the benign-absorption thesis to be right**: hyperscaler operating cash must keep carrying
most of the capex (issuance stays a term-out of strength, not a funding of necessity); order-book
depth must persist as supply compounds toward the ~$4T pipeline; the tech-premium erasure must
remain what it looks like so far — the market correctly CHARGING for supply, i.e., the system
working; and software stress must stay contained to equity values as Lincoln's marks suggest.
