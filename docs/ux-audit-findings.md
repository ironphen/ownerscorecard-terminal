# OWNERSCORECARD UX AUDIT — 45 FINDINGS

*Five design agents, 2026-07-25, reading the source and the built output rather than describing
screenshots. Lanes: the groupings browse redesign (to the owner's stated spec, filed separately in
the redesign section below), navigation and wayfinding, the company page, mobile and tables, and
the first-visit problem. Every finding names the file it lives in.*

*Status: FILED, NOT RATIFIED. These are recommendations. The house rule that constrains all of
them: no ratings, no verdicts, no number hidden behind an interaction, and a page that says
everything says nothing — removing a weak section beats adding a strong one.*

*Themes worth reading first:*
- *Several company-page sections render empty or near-empty for most companies (the Inversion
  panel is blank on 98.7% of the pages it appears on). The audit's strongest recommendation is
  subtraction, not addition.*
- *Three chart sections redraw rows of the ten-year table as pictures.*
- *No company page links to its own grouping table — zero of 3,872. The comparative surface and
  the record surface do not know about each other.*
- *One genuine correctness finding: two functions both claim to compute the record's median and
  do it differently. That is a doctrine violation, not a UX nit, and it is fixed first.*

### First screen · the home page makes no claim at all
- **Problem:** The home page carries no statement of what the publication is. Rendered, the entire above-the-fold copy is the masthead line "An independent research publication · records as of July 17, 2026", a search box placeholder ("Search 3,872 companies by name or ticker…"), six Try chips, and then the wire band. There is no h1 on the page at all (verified: zero <h1> in dist/client/index.html). The refusal that is the actual differentiator appears nowhere on the first screen.
- **Why:** A stranger's model after ten seconds is "a filings search box with an EDGAR feed under it", which is a category with dozens of free entrants and no reason to prefer this one. The one thing that cannot be copied cheaply, that the house refuses to pronounce, is invisible. The sentence that does the work already exists in the house's own voice, three times, all of them out of reach on a first visit: /about ("The publication carries no ratings, price targets, or estimates." — footer link only), /docs/data ("no ratings, no estimates, and no price data, by design: the publication presents the record and leaves the judgment to the reader" — a developer page), and the Founding Letter card dek ("never a rating, a price target, or a live quote"), which sits third in the last band, roughly two screens down at 13.5px.
- **Fix:** Add exactly two lines of type above the search box, no box, no panel, no illustration. Line one, serif, ~19px, and make it the page's h1: "A decade of every company's filed record, traced to its own filings." Line two, ~14px, muted: "No ratings, no price targets, no estimates, no price charts. Where a figure could not be read honestly, the reason is printed instead." That is the whole intervention on the home page: two sentences, one of which already exists verbatim in the house's own copy elsewhere. It adds no interaction, hides nothing, and scores nothing. It also fixes the missing h1.
- **Effort:** S (one block in src/pages/index.astro plus ~6 lines of scoped CSS) · **Risk:** Low. The only real risk is the line drifting into marketing register; keep it declarative and let it be lifted verbatim from /about so the voice is already ratified.

### The first substantive screen is a run of regional banks, by accident of a tiebreak
- **Problem:** The Fresh from the wire band sorts by significance, then date descending, then String(a.ticker).localeCompare(String(b.ticker)). In earnings season every row shares a date and a significance, so the tiebreak is pure alphabetical by ticker. Today's render is AXP, BAH, CHTR, COFS, CPF, DOW, FHB, FLG: ChoiceOne Financial Services, Central Pacific Financial Corp New, First Hawaiian, Flagstar Bank. Eight rows, half of them small regional banks, occupying the whole second half of the first screen.
- **Why:** This is the only content on the front door with real substance, and it currently reads as a random alphabetical slice. A serious stranger scanning for a name they recognize, the fastest possible route to a finished company page, finds nothing they know. It also quietly undersells coverage: the reader cannot tell from this that Apple, Berkshire and Toyota are in here.
- **Fix:** Change the final tiebreak from ticker-alphabetical to latest-year revenue descending. The house already owns this exact move and its justification, verbatim, on the groupings index: tables "open largest first by latest-year revenue, the one objective order." It is a filed fact, not a rank of quality, so it adds no score and pronounces nothing. Same eight rows, but a stranger sees American Express and Charter and Dow first.
- **Effort:** S (one comparator in the wireFeed sort) · **Risk:** Low. Confirm the revenue field is present for every pool that can reach this feed (the feed already filters to KNOWN US tickers, so it is); rows missing revenue should sort to the back rather than to the front, matching the grouping tables' own dash-sorts-last rule.

### "Next year contracted" is undefined vocabulary explaining itself with more undefined vocabulary
- **Problem:** The software family's second column is labelled "Next year contracted" with basis "obligations landing within 12 months ÷ revenue · withheld where the band is untagged". There is no entry for it in concepts.mjs (the glossary's 31 ids contain nothing for remaining performance obligations or backlog). The basis line asks the reader to already know what "obligations" means here, and "the band" is internal pipeline vocabulary that appears nowhere else a reader can reach. In the rendered table, Oracle, SAP, Adobe, Intuit and most of the tail show a bare em dash for this column, while the table legend defines a dash as "a figure that could not be read".
- **Why:** This is the house rule inverted. The rule is that a withheld figure shows its reason; here a withheld figure shows the same glyph as an unread one, and the only reason available sits in a column head written in terms the reader has not been given. Software is a heavily trafficked grouping and this is its most distinctive column.
- **Fix:** Two things. First, add one glossary entry ("Contracted revenue (remaining performance obligations)") to concepts.mjs and link the column head to it, saying plainly that it is revenue the company has already signed and expects to recognize within twelve months, that a high figure means next year is largely sold before it starts, and that it is only readable where the filer tags the current-portion band, so most filers show nothing. Second, distinguish the two dashes: render withheld cells with the site's withheld mark and a per-cell title naming the reason, and keep the plain dash for unread. If two glyphs is one too many, the smaller version is to change the legend so it names both cases honestly rather than collapsing them.
- **Effort:** M (one glossary entry, one link, one cell-render branch) · **Risk:** Medium on the two-glyph half: a second dash-like mark in a dense numeric table is exactly the kind of decoration the house rules out. The glossary entry and the link are unambiguously safe; ship those first and treat the glyph split as a separate decision.

### "Float" is used in two incompatible senses, one of them unlinked
- **Problem:** The glossary defines insurance-float (concepts.mjs line 205): funds an insurer holds but does not own. The first is a five-word deletion and is the smaller change.

### Groupings table — column widths (THE single change)
- **Problem:** C:/Users/ryanr/.surplus-scout/ownerscorecard-terminal/src/pages/groupings/[slug].astro has exactly one media query, `@media (min-width: 720px)`, and no narrow-screen tuning whatsoever. Column widths are set by the header, not the data: `table.grp th, table.grp td { white-space: nowrap }` applies to the sort button, so "Return on invested capital" at 11px/700/uppercase/0.04em renders ~190px on one line, and `table.grp thead .basis { max-width: 24ch; white-space: normal }` holds a ~125px floor under every numeric column. Measured on the built aerospace-and-defense page (10 columns, 63 rows) the numeric columns run ~145–210px each; the banks grouping carries 11 columns. Against a 390px phone the body content box is 362px (BaseLayout `padding: 0 14px` below 520px) and the frozen pane eats 122px (`.sel` 34 + `.tk` 88), leaving ~240px of moving surface — about 1.6 numeric columns. Reaching the last column takes four or five full swipes.
- **Why:** A grouping page is the terminal's comparison surface: it exists so an enterprising investor can run an eye down one column across a shelf. On a phone the eye can barely hold one column, and the reader is paying most of the screen for typography of the header rather than for figures. This is the largest gap in the product between what a reader came for and what the screen gives them.
- **Fix:** Add a `@media (max-width: 700px)` block to the page's style: (a) let the header label wrap — `table.grp thead th.num { max-width: 12ch }` and `table.grp thead button { white-space: normal; text-align: right; display: inline-block }`; (b) cap the basis line to match — `table.grp thead .basis { max-width: 12ch }`. The basis prose stays fully visible (provenance is not hidden), it just wraps to three or four lines in a header that is already sticky, so the cost is paid once vertically instead of on every column horizontally. Column width then falls back to the data cell ("$89.5B" ≈ 45px + 20px padding ≈ 65–85px). Combined with the name/industry fix below, the aerospace table goes from roughly 1,500px wide to roughly 700px, and the visible numeric columns go from ~1.6 to ~3. Do not touch the frozen ticker pane — that part is right.
- **Effort:** Small — one media block, ~6 declarations, no markup change, no JS. · **Risk:** Low. Header height grows to roughly 90px on a phone; verify the tallest header (banks, 11 columns) still leaves usable pane height inside `max-height: 82vh`. No data is hidden, no cell content changes, desktop is untouched above 700px.

### Groupings table — touch targets below the WCAG 2.2 minimum
- **Problem:** The grouping table's two interactive controls are both under 24×24 CSS px. Sort: `table.grp thead button { background: none; border: 0; padding: 0; margin: 0; …font-size: 11px }` (line 334) gives a target roughly 60×14px. Pick: `table.grp .sel input { accent-color: var(--accent); cursor: pointer; margin: 0 }` (line 310) with no size set renders ~13–16px inside a 34px column, and the surrounding `td.sel` is not a label, so the only live area is the box itself. The label keeps the existing `aria-label` semantics on the input. Neither change adds a control or a verdict; both enlarge an existing one.

## Specification

GROUPINGS BROWSE REDESIGN — CONCRETE SPECIFICATION

A. WHAT THE OWNER'S SPEC RESOLVES TO IN THIS CODEBASE

Since the 2026-07-17 standardization, one grouping IS one conventional industry (shelves.json: 93 shelves, each with exactly one industry, each carrying `sector` and `family`). B. THE THREE VIEWS AND THEIR URLS (every view is a real pre-rendered path; nothing is addressed by query string except sort)

1. /groupings/ — THE DOOR. Currently 93 entries stacked sector by sector, each with a name, a count, and a full column list. That page is the "scrolling down and going back and forth" the owner is complaining about, and it is roughly 400 lines tall. It becomes short:

Markup, in order:
  <nav class="pick"> containing

  Select 2, "Industry": on /groupings/ it is rendered but disabled with the single option "Choose a sector first". Refused, deliberately: filtering the table in place by hiding rows. It would put a number behind an interaction, it would break the ?sort URL contract, and it would require shipping the whole sector's rows to filter a subset out of them.

Rows: every member of every industry in the sector, across the US, ADR and JP pools, exactly the criterion [slug].astro uses today (presence on a shelf whose sector matches). Financials is the largest at 664 rows; Utilities the smallest at 120. Total across the 11 pages, 3,827 rows.

The group line: keep it, but on a sector page each fragment must carry the count of members it was read on, e.g. "median operating margin 12.4% (read on 604 of 657)". A median over a mixed sector is a weaker statement than over one industry, and the count is what keeps it honest. The industry chapters already state reads this way (chapterFacts), so there is precedent and no new form.

2. Freeze the sector column sets. The selection rule (section 2) reads answer rates off the live data, so a pipeline refresh could silently add or drop a column from a published page between builds. ## The hard problem: what columns an all-of-sector table shows

THE HARD PROBLEM: WHAT COLUMNS AN ALL-OF-SECTOR TABLE SHOWS

A. THE FACT THAT DECIDES IT

I measured this rather than reasoned about it. Two things came out of the data that change the shape of the answer.

First, family disagreement inside a sector is much rarer than the question implies. Five of eleven sectors are single-family and the problem does not arise at all: Materials (9 industries, all general), Consumer Discretionary (17, all general), Consumer Staples (8, all general), Utilities (4, all heavy), Real Estate (3, all property). Three more mix only general with heavy, which differ by two columns out of six. The genuinely hard cases are exactly three: Financials (lender + fee + insurer), Information Technology (software + semiconductor + general), and Health Care (general + one managedCare industry).

Second, and this is the load-bearing fact: groupingCells is already family-agnostic. cellFor is a switch on a column key; it will compute any key for any company, and it already knows the difference between a figure that cannot be READ ("—") and a figure that is a CATEGORY ERROR for that kind of business ("n/a", gated on financialKind / financialProfile / oiReliable). A column's absence from a family is therefore NOT evidence that the column is meaningless for those companies. Software companies have perfectly good gross margins; the software family drops gross margin because "next year contracted" earns the space, not because the figure is a lie. This means a lowest-common-denominator INTERSECTION of family column sets is the wrong instrument. It measures editorial crowding, not honesty. The Financials intersection is 3 columns; the IT intersection is 3; the Health Care intersection is 1 (revenue). One column is not a table.

B. THE ANSWER: THE SECTOR SET IS CHOSEN BY WHAT THE SECTOR'S ROWS CAN ANSWER, NOT BY WHAT ITS FAMILIES HAPPEN TO LIST

Rule, computed at build time, frozen into the source (see spec section E2):

  1. If every industry in the sector shares one family, the sector table shows that family's columns unchanged. No rule, no argument. (5 sectors.)
  2. Otherwise the candidates are the union of the member families' columns. A candidate is kept only if BOTH:
     (a) COVERAGE: the families that list it cover at least 40% of the sector's rows. This is the anti-tail test. It stops one small industry from dragging its specialist instrument onto a whole sector's table.
     (b) ANSWER RATE: it returns a sortable figure for at least two thirds of the sector's rows. This is the anti-dash test. A column that is mostly "—" or mostly "n/a" is a column of holes, and a table of holes is worse than a narrower table.
  3. Cap at 7 (the FAMILIES comment's own standard: "5-7 columns per family, dense enough to compare, never a wall"), ranked by coverage then answer rate, but RENDERED in the canonical COL declaration order so tables read the same way across sectors.
  4. Hand amendments are permitted, one line each, with the reason recorded in the file. The rule generates a defensible default; it does not get the last word.

C. WHAT THE RULE ACTUALLY PRODUCES (measured, 3,827 rows)

  Energy (166, general+heavy):        Revenue | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC | Net debt
      gross margin dropped at 48% answered, which is the same reason the heavy family already drops it
  Materials (245, single):            Revenue | Gross margin | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC | Net debt
  Industrials (657, general+heavy):   Revenue | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC | Gross margin | Net debt
  Consumer Discretionary (444):       Revenue | Gross margin | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC | Net debt
  Consumer Staples (140):             Revenue | Gross margin | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC | Net debt
  Health Care (495, +managedCare):    Revenue | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC | Net debt
      gross margin dropped at 61% (biotech cost lines); managed care's premiums / MLR / reserve development
      fail coverage at 2% of rows and stay in the Managed Care table where they belong
  Financials (664, lender+fee+insurer): Revenue | Net income | Return on tangible equity | Tangible equity
  Information Technology (487, software+semi+general): Revenue | Gross margin | Stock pay | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC
  Communication Services (194):       Revenue | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC | Net debt
  Utilities (120, single):            Revenue | Operating margin | Owner earnings | ROIC | Net debt | Dividends paid
  Real Estate (215, single):          Revenue | Funds from operations | Dividends paid | Net debt | Total assets

Every column on every sector table answers for at least 70% of that sector's rows. Lowest is IT's Stock pay at 71% and Real Estate's FFO at 70%; most are above 90%.

D. WHY THIS IS RIGHT, ARGUED

The Financials result is the proof. Four columns fall out: Revenue, Net income, Return on tangible equity, Tangible equity, each answering 94-97% of 664 rows. That is exactly, and only, how you can honestly compare a bank to a P&C insurer to an exchange: what it took in, what it earned, the hard capital behind it, and the return on that capital. Deposits (46% answered) and premiums (19%) and float (12%) correctly do not appear, because on a sector table they would each be a column that is blank for four fifths of the page. Operating margin correctly does not appear: it is n/a for 67% of Financials rows by construction. The rule found the four-column table a careful editor would have hand-built, and it found it by refusing to print holes.

The IT result is the second proof. All the loud specialist columns fall away: "Next year contracted" answers 9% of IT rows, R&D intensity 72% but only for a fifth of the sector, inventory days for the two semiconductor industries alone. What survives is the universal operating read. That is the correct division of labour and it is the real design idea here: THE SECTOR TABLE IS A DIFFERENT INSTRUMENT FROM THE INDUSTRY TABLE, NOT A BIGGER ONE. At sector scale you are asking "how big, how profitable, how much capital, how much debt" across many kinds of business. At industry scale you are asking "which of these software companies has more of next year already contracted." The second select is not merely a filter; it is a change of instrument, and that is precisely why it earns its place rather than being a convenience.

E. THE THREE OPTIONS I REJECTED, AND WHY

Lowest common denominator (intersection of family column sets): rejected. It measures editorial crowding rather than what the figures can honestly say, and it produces a one-column Health Care table and a three-column IT table. It also silently penalises a sector for having one well-curated small industry in it.

Per-industry sub-grouping inside the sector view (banded rows with a repeated header per family): rejected as a default. It destroys the one thing a comparative table is for, which is running an eye down a single column across the whole page, and it makes whole-table sorting incoherent. The reader can have the banding for free anyway: the Industry column is a sortable text column, and one click on it groups the sector into contiguous industry blocks without any new machinery, any new markup, or any loss of sortability. State that in the page's prose and it costs nothing.

A sector-level table of MEDIANS, one row per sector, on the index page: rejected. It would be arithmetic over filed figures and therefore defensible on the letter of the house style, but a sortable grid of eleven sectors by median return is the closest thing to a ranking this site could print, and it would be read as one within a week. The index table stays inventory only: sector, industries, companies, and the columns its table shows.

F. THE ONE AMENDMENT I RECOMMEND, AND ONE I FLAG

Recommend: Information Technology gains NET DEBT, giving it seven columns. It answers 97% of IT rows and is a category error for none of them. It fails only the coverage test (23%), and it fails it for a purely editorial reason: the software and semiconductor families spent their column budget elsewhere. Debt net of the cash against it is close to the most Graham column on the board, and refusing to print it on the sector-wide table because two families were crowded is the rule outranking the judgment it was meant to serve.

Flag for the owner's decision, not asserted: Financials gaining TOTAL ASSETS. It answers 97% of Financials rows and is the size measure a lender is genuinely read on, and revenue for a bank is a reconstruction. Against it: total assets appears in no Financials family today, only in the property family, so adding it is a new curation claim about how these businesses are read rather than a rearrangement of existing ones. That is the owner's call, and the reason should be written into the file either way.


## Static-build constraints

STATIC BUILD: WHAT IS PRE-RENDERED, WHAT JS RUNS, WHAT A CRAWLER AND A NO-JS READER GET

A. NOTHING IN THIS DESIGN NEEDS A SERVER, AND NOTHING NEEDS CLIENT-SIDE DATA

Every view is a path that Astro pre-renders. /groupings/sector/[slug].astro exports getStaticPaths over the 11 distinct sector values in shelves.json. No client-side row store, no fetch, no JSON payload, no hydrated component. The rows, the cells, the withholdings, the group line and both select option lists are all rendered at build time. The output is HTML.

B. THE JS BUDGET, EXACTLY

Existing, unchanged, already on every grouping table page:
  - the sort script, about 60 lines inline, plain DOM re-ordering of rows already present, mirrors ?sort= and &dir= into the address bar with history.replaceState.
  - the compare picker, about 35 lines inline, builds a /compare?c= link out of two to four checked rows.
New:
  - the control bar script, about 20 lines inline. It does exactly three things: remove the `hidden` attribute from the two selects, hide the link rail, and add a change listener on each select that does location.assign(select.value). No state, no storage, no data.

Total new JavaScript is under 1KB, inline, no imports, no framework. The site already uses `is:inline` scripts of this size on this exact page, so this introduces no new pattern.

C. WHAT A READER WITH JS DISABLED GETS

He gets a working browse, not a degraded one, because the link rail is in the HTML and is only hidden by the script that makes the selects live. With JS off the script never runs, the selects stay `hidden`, and the rail stays visible. He never sees a dead control. This is the whole reason the selects ship hidden rather than being hidden by CSS or shown by default.

Concretely with JS off:
  - /groupings/ shows the 11 sector links as a rail, the 11-row sector table, and the dense 93-item industry index.
  - a sector page shows "All sectors" plus that sector's industries as links, and the full sector table with every Industry cell linking to that industry's table. Nothing is hidden and no row is missing.
  - an industry page shows the crumb, the sibling industries as links, and the table exactly as today.
What he loses: the sort controls (already true today, the headers are buttons that do nothing without JS, and the server-rendered largest-first-by-revenue order still holds), and the compare picker (already true today).

D. WHAT A CRAWLER GETS

Eleven new indexable pages, each with a distinct <title>, a distinct description naming the sector, its company count and its column list, and a canonical self-URL. Every link that matters is a real <a href> in the source, including the ones the script later hides, so the link graph is intact whether or not the crawler executes JS. The @astrojs/sitemap integration picks the new routes up automatically and they inherit the same honest fundamentals asOf lastmod the config already computes rather than a build clock.

Crawl depth is the one thing to watch. Today all 93 industry tables are one hop from /groupings/. If the index is shortened without replacement they become two hops. That is precisely why the dense alphabetical 93-item index stays at the bottom of the door page: it is about ten lines of rendered height and it preserves the one-hop link graph for every table on the site.

Duplicate-content risk is low but real: /groupings/sector/information-technology/ and /groupings/software/ share 135 of 487 rows. They differ in column set, in framing prose, in title and in description, and neither is a paginated variant of the other, so no canonical cross-reference is warranted. Do not add one.

E. BUILD AND WEIGHT

Eleven additional pages. The heaviest is Financials at 664 rows by 8 columns, which lands at roughly 500KB of raw HTML. Measured gzip ratio on an existing dense page of this site (dist/client/almanac/index.html, 134,517 bytes raw to 23,595 gzipped) is 5.7x, so Financials arrives at roughly 85-90KB over the wire. That makes it the heaviest page on the site, and worth knowing, but it is one document with no blocking script and no client data, and the existing Banks table at 289 rows already ships today without complaint. No truncation, no pagination, no "show more". Hiding rows behind an interaction is forbidden by the house style and would also break the sort contract.

Build time cost is one extra classify-and-cells pass over the three pools, the same work [slug].astro already does 93 times.

F. THE SORT URL CONTRACT ON THE NEW PAGES

Unchanged and inherited verbatim. ?sort={column key}&dir=asc|desc, the default order keeps a clean URL, state lives in the address bar and nowhere else. A sector URL carrying a sort key that does not exist in that sector's column set (someone edits ?sort=deposits onto the Financials sector URL) simply finds no matching header, the boot loop exits without a match, and the page stays in its default order. That is already the existing behaviour and needs no new code.


## Mobile

ON A PHONE

A. THE CONTROL BAR IS THE PART OF THIS DESIGN THAT PHONES ACTUALLY WIN ON

Two native selects are the single best control a phone has. iOS and Android render them as a full-screen wheel or sheet with the current value pre-selected, which is a better list picker than anything built in HTML, and the lists are small: 11 sectors, and at most 17 industries (Consumer Discretionary), most sectors fewer. The bar stacks to two full-width rows under 560px, with labels above rather than beside.

One non-negotiable detail: the selects must be at least 16px font-size. Below 16px, iOS Safari zooms the viewport on focus and then does not zoom back out, which leaves the reader stranded at 1.4x on a table page. The site's sans stack at 12px is the house density and it is wrong here specifically.

B. THE HONEST ANSWER ABOUT THE SECTOR TABLE ON A PHONE

The sector table is a laptop surface. A 664-row by 8-column table on a 390px screen is a thing you scroll, not a thing you read. I am not going to pretend otherwise, and I am not going to propose a card layout, a collapsed row, or a "tap to expand" cell, because all three hide a number behind an interaction and are wrong by construction here.

What makes this acceptable rather than a failure is that the phone reader has an escape that the current design does not give him: the second select. On a phone the sector table's job is to be the place you land and immediately narrow from. That is not a consolation prize, it is the same instrument argument from section 2, and it happens to be exactly what a small screen wants.

C. WHAT SURVIVES UNCHANGED

The existing frozen-pane treatment carries over verbatim and is already well built for this. The table scrolls sideways inside .tableWrap so the page body never scrolls sideways; the header row is sticky at the top; the pick box and Ticker are sticky at the left with their own opaque backgrounds; the Name column joins the frozen pane only above 720px, which is the right call and already in the CSS. tbody tr scroll-margin-top: 76px keeps a #TICKER deep link clear of the sticky header. None of this needs touching for sector pages.

D. THE ONE THING I WOULD CHANGE, AND THE CSS LIMIT BEHIND IT

.tableWrap sets max-height: 82vh with overflow: auto, which creates a nested vertical scroller inside a scrolling page. On a laptop with a 289-row table that is fine. On a phone with a 664-row table it is the classic two-axis scroll trap: the reader's vertical swipe is captured by the inner box, the page behind it will not move, and reaching the footnote requires scrolling 664 rows first.

The obvious fix does not exist in CSS. Setting overflow-x: auto with overflow-y: visible is not honoured; a non-visible value on one axis computes the other to auto. So the choice is real:

Recommended: under 720px, keep overflow-x on the wrap for the columns and set max-height to none. The wrap then computes overflow-y to auto but has no height constraint, so it never scrolls vertically on its own and the page scrolls normally. The thead stays position: sticky and sticks against the viewport instead of against the wrap, which is the behaviour you want on a phone anyway. This needs a device check on iOS Safari, where sticky inside a horizontally scrollable ancestor has historically been the flakiest case on the platform. If sticky headers turn out not to hold, the fallback is to let the header scroll away on phones and rely on the frozen Ticker column plus the industry select, which is a smaller loss than trapping the scroll.

E. TWO SMALLER PHONE POINTS

The compare picker's 34px checkbox column is below the 44px touch target minimum. It works, it is just fiddly, and it is worth widening the padded hit area without widening the visual column. This is pre-existing, not introduced here.

The group line on a sector page will run to several fragments each carrying a member count. Let it wrap normally at 13.5px serif; do not truncate it. It is prose, not a control.

Measured: 'Next year contracted' answers 22% of Software (135 rows), 3% of IT Services & Consulting (85 rows), 5% of Communications Equipment (39 rows). 'Sales & marketing' answers 77% / 30% / 35%. So two of the three industries on the software family are looking at a table whose two distinguishing columns are mostly dashes, and they would arguably be better served by the general family. Separately, 22% on Software itself means the RPO keyhole parser reaches roughly one member in five, so the desk's flagship column is empty for four fifths of the industry it was built for. Both are pre-existing and neither blocks the redesign, but the sector rule will make them visible and the owner should decide rather than discover.
- The EU pool is invisible to this whole surface. src/data/fundamentals.eu.json holds 45 companies as of 2026-07-17 and is read by index.astro, catalog.astro, almanac, docs/data, company-index.json.js and /eu/[ticker], but NOT by groupings/index.astro, groupings/[slug].astro or industries/[slug].astro, all three of which read only us, adr and jp. The new sector pages will inherit the omission, so a sector count on /groupings/ will disagree with the same sector's count on /catalog. Either bring the EU pool into the groupings pipeline as part of this work or state the three-pool scope explicitly on the page; do not ship a fourth surface that silently drops 45 records.
- The net-debt withholding guard keys off the TABLE's family, not the row's. groupingCells checks `familyKey !== "property"` before allowing a net-debt figure. If a sector page passes a synthetic sector family key, every REIT's net debt becomes n/a and the Real Estate sector table loses a column that actually answers for 91% of its rows. I confirmed this empirically. The refactor to cellFor(key, company, terms, rowFamilyKey) is a hard prerequisite, not a cleanup.
- Data-driven column sets can silently rewrite a published page. The sector selection rule reads answer rates off the live pools, so a pipeline refresh that improves or degrades one tag could add or remove a column from a sector table between two builds with no human in the loop, and a reader's bookmarked ?sort=grossMargin URL would quietly stop resolving. Mitigate exactly as the codebase already mitigates slugs: compute once, freeze the result as a literal SECTOR_COLUMNS map with the measured figures in the comment, and add a drift test to npm test that fails when the rule's recomputed output no longer matches the frozen set.
- Whole industries will read n/a across a sector table, and it will look like the table failing them unless the prose says otherwise. Roughly ten Managed Care rows answer only Revenue among the five Health Care sector columns; 29% of Real Estate rows are non-REITs whose FFO is a category error; 84% of Financials rows are n/a on ROIC, owner earnings, gross margin and net debt, which is why those columns are not in the Financials set at all. This is the house style working correctly, but a reader meeting a row of n/a without an explanation reads it as a gap in the data rather than a refusal, and the two are opposite claims.
- Page weight and crawl depth both move. Financials at 664 rows lands near 500KB raw, roughly 85-90KB gzipped at this site's measured 5.7x ratio, making it the heaviest document on the site. And shortening the index from 93 entries to 11 pushes every industry table from one hop to two unless the dense 93-item alphabetical index stays at the bottom of /groupings/. Keep it.
- The client sort has no explicit tiebreak, so it is only as stable as the browser's sort and the current DOM order. The server-rendered default breaks revenue ties alphabetically by ticker, but once a reader sorts by Industry and then back to Revenue, the tie order is whatever the previous pass left. Harmless today at 289 rows; more visible at 664, where sorting by Industry is the recommended way to band the sector and will be used. Add ticker as an explicit secondary key.
- The compare picker will now be able to select across archetypes, for example a bank against an exchange against a P&C insurer, because a sector table puts them in one list. /compare presumably handles mixed archetypes since company pages already link head-to-heads, but it has never been reachable this easily before. Verify the four-way mixed-archetype case renders withheld rather than blank or misaligned before shipping.
- Route collision guard is missing. /groupings/sector/{slug} sits under the same directory as /groupings/{industry-slug}. No industry is slugged 'sector' today, but nothing prevents a future taxonomy edit from creating one, and the failure would be a silently shadowed route rather than a build error. Add it to the existing import-time guard in groupingColumns.mjs, which already fails the build on duplicate slugs and unknown families.
