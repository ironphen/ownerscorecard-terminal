# Organized Owners — an exploration

*2026-07-18. Status: exploration, not a plan. No build decision has been made. This memo exists so
the thinking survives the conversation that produced it.*

The question, as Ryan framed it across two conversations: individual shareholding is fading the way
Munger warned — annual meetings gone virtual, retail not voting, institutions holding the votes,
boards and executives helping themselves — and the trend runs decades. Could OwnerScorecard evolve
into something that lets individual shareholders organize in the 21st century — not a place to
speculate (StockTwits, Reddit already exist) but a place to **behave as owners**?

Everything below rests on two adversarially verified research rounds (11 lanes total; workflow
outputs `wf_11c5f6c9-8fc` and `wf_09c541fa-edc`). Load-bearing numbers were checked against primary
sources; where a figure is secondhand it is marked.

---

## 1. The trend is real, and worse than the lament

- Retail voted **28.0% of its shares in 2025 — a nine-year low** (institutions 76.6%, itself a
  ten-year low). Only ~12% of retail *accounts* vote at all; participation rises with stake, which
  means the cause is rational cost-benefit, not indifference (Brav/Cain/Zytnick, JFE 2022).
- Direct household control of US equities fell from roughly 92% (1950) to a minority; the Big Three
  index managers cast ~25–28% of S&P 500 votes. Munger, DJCO 2022, verbatim: *"We have a new bunch
  of emperors, and they're the people who vote the shares in the index funds... I'm not sure I want
  him to be my emperor."*
- Say-on-pay fails ~1.4% of the time while median S&P 500 CEO pay **doubled in the say-on-pay era**
  ($9.3M in 2011 → $17.1M in 2024). Buffett named the mechanism in 2005: *"Ratchet, Ratchet and
  Bingo."*
- 70.7% of S&P 500 meetings remain virtual-only; measured effect: 17% shorter meetings, and of 390
  shareholder questions submitted at virtual-only meetings, 36% were answered — suppression
  concentrated exactly where votes ran against management (Schwartz-Ziv, ECGI).
- **The live threat**: ExxonMobil obtained SEC no-action relief (Sept 15, 2025) for standing voting
  instructions that auto-vote retail shares WITH the board, debuting this proxy season; copycats
  expected; As You Sow and a pension fund are already contesting the asymmetry. Management is
  capturing the passive-retail rail first. If no independent default gets built, the board's rail
  becomes THE retail default.

## 2. Why every existing venue converges to speculation

Not the users — the architecture. Four mechanisms, each invertible:

1. **The atom is the post.** Threads die in 48 hours; ownership is measured in years. When the unit
   of conversation is ephemeral, content that changes hourly wins, and the only thing about a
   business that changes hourly is its price.
2. **Status accrues for being right fast.** Upvotes reward entertainment and quick calls (WSB
   ritualized gain/loss screenshots). No venue accrues status for being right slowly.
3. **Identity is cheap.** Anonymous, position-optional, tenure-free. Cheap identity → cheap talk →
   loudest wins. InvestorsHub (free + anonymous) rotted into a pump swamp; Silicon Investor's
   $200 pay-to-post stayed cleaner for years — the gate was the culture.
4. **Price is the campfire.** StockTwits organizes conversation around the ticker as a stream of
   takes; the chart is the shared object. If the campfire is a price feed, the conversation is
   speculation.

**The inversion is OSC's existing refusal.** A community whose shared objects are the record and
the filing moves at the speed the businesses move. StockTwits/Reddit cannot copy this: removing
velocity would kill their engagement economics. This is the second time OSC's differentiation takes
the form of a refusal the incumbents cannot afford (the first: no verdicts vs the ratings sites).

## 3. What already exists (round-1 landscape, verified)

- **Rails: built and commoditized.** Broadridge pass-through voting reaches 600+ funds / $8T
  (Mar 2026); Vanguard's retail voting-choice program has 82k participants — **~1–2% of eligible**.
  Canned policy menus don't move retail. Plumbing was never the bottleneck people assumed; the
  judgment layer is.
- **Occupied cells**: shareholder Q&A (Say/Robinhood, $140M); ESG-values proxy advice (iconik +
  As You Sow); institutional AI voting (JPMorgan dropped ISS entirely for in-house "Proxy IQ,"
  Jan 2026; Wells Fargo followed; Glass Lewis kills its house recommendation in 2027; a Dec 2025
  executive order is re-bidding the whole proxy-advice layer).
- **Verifiably empty**: the independent, owner-oriented (business-quality, Graham-Buffett)
  information layer for the individual voter; and the retail "clerk reads the proxy against YOUR
  principles" cell (exists only as an MIT/Yale paper, arXiv 2510.23475).

## 4. The 20th-century answer: shareholder associations (round 2)

Europe and Australia kept living national shareholder associations; the US did not.

| Body | Members | What members get | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aktiespararna (SE, 1966) | ~70k (peak 147k in 2000) | magazine, 100+ local chapters, ~450 AGMs attended/yr, education, owns a broker | halved from peak, stable |
| DSW (DE, 1947) | ~30k + 7,000 clubs | **votes members' deposited proxies at ~650–700 AGMs/yr** (AktG §135 hard-codes associations as proxy agents), litigation (Wirecard €700M claim) | aging |
| VEB (NL, 1924) | ~35k | securities litigation (Fortis €1.3bn, Ahold €1.1bn, Shell $380M) via Dutch collective-settlement law | shrinking |
| ASA (AU, 1960) | 6,836 | **standing proxies lodged at the registry**: 25,935 proxies worth A$2.98bn voted in 2024; 146 companies monitored by 115 volunteers | ran a A$218k deficit on A$1.3M revenue; ~47% of membership-type revenue now comes from the companies it monitors |
| ShareSoc/UKSA (UK) | 9.5k / a few hundred | campaigns, voting guidance; UKSA arranges private director meetings | UKSA: £17,771 turnover, zero employees |

**Why they survived where the US's United Shareholders Association (1986–93) died:** they sell
annually renewing *services* (AGM representation, magazines, claims recovery, clubs) rather than a
finite cause; European law hard-codes them into the system (German §135 proxy agency, Dutch
collective settlements) while the US contingency-fee class action does the recovery job for free;
and small national markets (~650 German listcos) make volunteer AGM coverage feasible. USA-1986 was
a patron-funded deregulation lobby — it won its reforms and dissolved.

**The condition they're all in**: aging, shrinking, sub-€2M budgets, weak digital presence,
diversifying into corporate money. The model works and is dying of demographics. **Nobody has
rebuilt the shareholder association as a digital-native organism.** That is the empty macro-cell.

## 5. The US evidence: what moves individuals, what doesn't

- **Education never crossed into ownership.** AAII (revenue −26% in three years) and
  BetterInvesting (400k+ members in ~1999 → 161,670 by 2006 → low tens of thousands today) never
  touched governance. Teaching people about stocks does not produce owners.
- **A shared specific stake does.** The Association of BellTel Retirees — a single-company
  association of individuals — won **11 governance changes at Verizon, including three majority
  votes**. The strongest US proof that organized individuals can win when organized around one
  company they actually own.
- **A concrete ownership ritual does, at scale.** The Superstonk DRS movement moved ~200,000 people
  to direct-register ~76.6M GameStop shares (25.1% of outstanding at peak, per the company's own
  filings) and hold for 4+ years — organic, unpaid, sustained. Single-company and squeeze-motivated,
  but the existence proof: give individuals a concrete *act of ownership* to perform together and
  they will perform it.
- **We The Investors** (2022–, active) organized ~71k signatures and thousands of SEC comment
  letters from retail on market structure — advocacy works at moments, but produces no durable
  daily organism.

## 6. The legal map (settled by round 2)

**The publisher lane is wide open.** Publishing vote research; announcing your own votes with
reasons (expressly *not* solicitation, 14a-1(l)(2)(iv)); polling and publishing aggregated reader
vote intentions; running a pre-meeting shareholder forum (14a-2(b)(6) + operator immunity under
14a-17); helping readers file their own 14a-8 proposals — all with **zero SEC filings** under
$5M held per issuer. ISS v. SEC (D.C. Cir., July 2025) put advice-not-solicitation on
constitutional-adjacent footing. Lowe v. SEC shields bona fide publishers from adviser registration.

**The association-proxy lane (ASA model) is effectively barred in the US.** Collecting members'
proxy appointments IS solicitation with no usable exemption (14a-2(b)(1) is expressly unavailable
to anyone seeking power to act as proxy) → full Schedule 14A per issuer per meeting; Rule
14a-4(d)(2)-(3) bars standing proxies outright (one-meeting rule); aggregated voting power counts
toward 13D beneficial ownership; and street-name plumbing means beneficial holders can't lodge
registry-level proxies anyway (what killed Moxy Vote). Even ASA, with a friendly legal regime, runs
on 26k proxies and loses money. **Conclusion: the publisher/community shape is not a fallback; it
is the design.** The one horizon door: the Exxon no-action letter invites consultation for
different structures — a *neutral* standing-voting-instruction program (reader-directed, not
board-aligned) is untested and already demanded by As You Sow's reconsideration request. Long game,
with counsel, if ever.

## 7. Community mechanics: the casebook

Forty years of investing communities, distilled:

- **Motley Fool boards (1994–2022)** — the closest working ancestor: per-ticker boards with decade
  memory, a **daily rec limit** (scarcity kept recommendations meaningful), rec-to-post-ratio
  trophies (quality per unit of talk, the exact anti-velocity currency), a penalty box, named
  regulars. Died of business-model starvation (boards never monetized; picks did) and then platform
  migration that **deleted decades of archives** — the community's memory, destroyed by its host.
- **Value Investors Club (2000–)** — alive at ~500 active members: work-sample admission (~1 in 15),
  obligations (2–6 ideas/yr, rate 20 others), delayed free public access. Gating produces quality
  and freezes scale. It is an *ideas* club — pitches about buying — not an owners' club.
- **COBF (2002–)** — 3–4k members, $50 lifetime, one owner-moderator, politics quarantined to a
  subdomain, anchored on two unpromotable mega-caps. Non-promotional because there are no engagement
  economics to feed.
- **MicroCapClub (2011–)** — peer-voted admission judged on writing quality; **monetizes readers
  (subscriber tier), never posters**. Quality holds.
- **Commonstock (2017–23)** — solved trust via brokerage-verified portfolios ($10B connected), never
  found a business, sold to Yahoo. Verification is a feature, not a company.
- **Silicon Investor vs InvestorsHub** — the controlled experiment: pay-to-post stayed civil;
  free-and-anonymous became the pump swamp whose operator faced SEC action.

Distilled rules: gate posting, not reading. Status from quality-per-post and tenure, rate-limited
by design. Archives are sacred and permanent (OSC's record-permanence doctrine already says this).
Anchor conversation on objects that cannot be pumped. Verify what needs verifying without making
verification the product. Monetize subscription/readers, never engagement.

## 8. The design primitives (what survives all of the above)

1. **Filings, not price.** The campfire is the record and the wire. No price feed, no charts, no
   daily thread; price talk out of bounds by rule. This is the moat's enforcement mechanism and it
   is structurally uncopyable by velocity-funded incumbents.
2. **The atom is the holding** — a (person × business × time) object: "owner/follower since 2019,
   here is my memo, here are my dated notes as each annual landed." The Owner's Notebook (S1/S2,
   shipped) is the single-player version of exactly this atom. The community is the notebook made
   selectively public; rooms are the aggregation of public notebook pages around the permanent
   record page. Solo value before network value — the bootstrap is already built.
3. **Status from stewardship behaviors, never outcomes.** Tenure, memos written, annuals read,
   votes declared, questions asked. Compounds, never decays — like ownership. Outcome-grading
   (returns leaderboards) is verdict machinery and re-selects for speculation; refused.
4. **Identity: attested tenure, never size.** "Holder since 2021," honor-system with public stats
   (the minigame-integrity model), consistent with the Notebook's device-local privacy and the
   positions-never-announced culture.
5. **Rituals make owners.** The evidence (ASA's AGM season, DRS, the Berkshire meeting) says
   individuals act as owners when given concrete, synchronized ownership acts. The calendar exists:
   proxy season (the Owner's Ballot as the room's centerpiece — items presented verdict-free beside
   the record), annual-report season (the room reads the annual together), and possibly DRS-like
   registration as a personal ownership act. Voting re-enters here — as ritual, not plumbing.
6. **The clerk.** The reader's AI clerk reads the proxy against the reader's saved principles and
   drafts *their* reasoning; they vote themselves on ProxyVote. OSC never recommends, never votes,
   never carries a ballot. (The Machine Readers lane, pointed at the one filing type OSC doesn't
   yet present.)
7. **Boring by design is the filter.** Speculators leave because there is nothing to eat. That is
   the moderation strategy, ahead of any rulebook.

## 9. Shapes, smallest first (none green-lit)

- **Step 0 — the Owner's Ballot page.** Proxy items presented verdict-free beside the relevant
  record (comp beside the pay-vs-record panel; directors beside their ownership and attendance from
  the proxy). Pure presentation surface, zero legal exposure, buildable inside the current mandate.
  Doubles as the cheapest possible probe of whether readers care.
- **Step 1 — notebook-public.** A member may publish chosen notebook pages (their owner's memo per
  business, dated notes). The first social object, derived from a shipped private feature.
- **Step 2 — rooms.** Per-business owners' rooms aggregating public notebooks + wire events +
  seasonal rituals; tenure-weighted standing; no price surface anywhere.
- **Step 3 — the register.** Opt-in declared votes; OSC publishes the aggregate tally per item
  ("readers of this record intend: N% against") under the forum rules. The community-of-records
  with civic teeth.
- **Horizon — the neutral standing-instructions door** (Exxon path, reader-directed). Counsel-gated,
  partner-gated, years out, optional.

**Standing refusals**: no house vote recommendations, ever; no OSC-cast votes; no vote carrying; no
vote markets; no campaigns; no returns leaderboards; no price surfaces in community spaces; archives
permanent.

## 10. Red team (kept honest)

- **"Take back corporate power" is an overclaim.** Retail is ~29% of street-name shares and votes
  *with* management on pay more than institutions do. The achievable scope: make the franchise real
  for the engaged tail, and build the independent default before the board-aligned one wins.
- **Uptake evidence is sobering.** Vanguard's beautiful rails: 1–2%. The bet is that canned
  policies aren't principles and menus aren't membership — unproven until probed.
- **Cold start.** Rooms need density. Sequencing answer: notebook-public before rooms; possibly one
  seeded room (the pool's most owner-cultured base) before many.
- **Moderation is doctrine-critical.** A room with OSC's name hosting pump-talk contaminates the
  publication. The no-price rule must be constitutional, enforced by architecture (nothing to pump)
  before moderators.
- **Economics.** This is the retention/community moat for the notes subscription (its ratified
  role in the phase-2 model), not a standalone revenue line. The Motley Fool lesson: if the
  community isn't load-bearing for the business model, it gets starved. Here it is load-bearing.

## 11. The accountability thesis (added same day, Ryan's escalation)

Ryan's furthest articulation: OSC as a *contributor to management accountability itself* — the
activist-investor function, decentralized to every company, through smarter individual owners
acting on their own volition. End state: a world of smarter owners, and therefore managements that
act in shareholders' interests more often.

**The mechanism, stated precisely: deterrence through legibility, not intervention.** The activist
investor's economics force concentration — one company, a big stake, campaigns that cost tens of
millions (Exxon/Engine No. 1 reportedly $35–50M). What CAN be decentralized is not the campaign
but the *watching*. OSC's pipeline already reads every filing of ~3,900 companies mechanically; the
record pages already render capital allocation, dilution, acquisitions, and pay beside delivered
results. The accountability contribution is not that OSC prosecutes anyone — it is that a
permanent, legible, owner-oriented record, visibly watched by an organized readership, changes
managerial behavior *before the fact*. The evidence that being watched works: pay-for-luck
concentrates in firms WITHOUT a monitoring blockholder (Bertrand-Mullainathan); concentrated
ownership raises pay-for-performance and lowers pay levels (Hartzell-Starks); and USA's
"Shareholder 1000" got 25 of 43 targeted companies to negotiate governance changes with no proxy
fight — the *ranking* was the weapon.

**The keystone refinement — why OSC's form of disclosure disciplines instead of ratcheting.**
Disclosure alone has a documented failure mode: the 1992 executive-pay disclosure reforms fed the
ratchet, because peer-relative visibility lets every board pay "above median" (Ratchet, Ratchet
and Bingo; Bizjak/Faulkender-Yang peer-group bias). Legibility of pay LEVELS inflates pay.
**Legibility of pay BESIDE THE DELIVERED RECORD is the anti-ratchet form**: the comparison object
is not what other CEOs make but what this management produced for owners. That is exactly the
juxtaposition OSC already builds, and it is the specific civic technology this thesis rests on.
Peer-relative disclosure ratchets; record-relative disclosure disciplines.

**The role separation that keeps it doctrine-true (and legally clean):** OSC is the record room
and the courthouse — never the prosecutor, never the judge. The publication renders; members
conclude; the register aggregates *their* conclusions ("owners of record here intend N% against"),
scrupulously accurately (14a-9 always applies). "United against bad management practices" is the
members' own volition, visible in aggregate; OSC itself never names a management good or bad.
Arrangement-is-a-pronouncement gets MORE binding here, not less.

**Honest limits.** (a) Deterrence is unmeasurable — the comp package never proposed appears in no
dashboard; this is a mission, not a metric, and must not be sold as a near-term product promise.
(b) It emerges patchily: per-company, where a dense engaged owner base exists (the BellTel proof),
not uniformly across 3,900 names. (c) Retail currently votes WITH management on pay more than
institutions do — "smarter" must precede "united," which is exactly the product's teaching function
(literacy by use). (d) If it works, issuers respond: IR courtship of rooms, astroturf retail-voice
orgs (Main Street Investors Coalition precedent), and management-aligned auto-vote rails (Exxon
SVI, live now). (e) **The capture trap is the deepest risk**: ASA now takes ~47% of its
membership-type revenue from the companies it monitors. OSC's reader-only revenue doctrine is the
constitutional armor against exactly this, and becomes non-negotiable the moment the accountability
function exists.

Mission sentence candidate: *a world with smarter owners — and, because of them, managements more
often worth owning.*

**Mission ratified (same day).** Ryan's words: *"re-align managements with shareholders through
literacy and organization of the individual enterprising investor re-establishing themselves as
owners of the business as was intended in our capitalistic system / stock ownership in general."*
"Enterprising investor" is Graham's own category, and the mission turns out to be Graham's own
project — stated in Forbes 1932 ("ownership conscious"), in Security Analysis 1934 (the "docile
and apathetic animal," tied by Graham himself to Berle-Means in the same paragraph), practiced in
the Northern Pipe Line campaign of 1926-28, preached in the 1946 lectures ("The trouble with
stockholders... is that not enough of them are disgruntled"), and pronounced unfinished in the
1973 Intelligent Investor ("In the last 36 years practically nothing has actually been
accomplished through intelligent action by the great body of shareholders"). The verified,
citation-grade quote pack for all of it: **`docs/graham-owner-factbase.md`**. Munger supplied the
warning; Graham supplied the mission; the design lens was always pointing here.
