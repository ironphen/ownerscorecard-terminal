## The verbatim quote pack (transcript page cites, read from page images)

- p. 518 (oral summary): "leading industrial stocks are not basically overvalued, but they are
  definitely not cheap; and they are in danger of going over into an unduly high level."
- p. 530: "Quantitatively the market seems to be about right, but qualitatively I consider it to
  be on the high side and getting into a dangerous situation."
- p. 545 (prepared statement): "common stocks look high and are high, but they are not as high as
  they look" and "The true measure of common stock values, of course, is not found by reference
  to price movements alone, but by price in relation to earnings, dividends, future prospects
  and, to a small extent, asset values."
- p. 546: his central-value method put the Dow near 396 against an actual ~410 — the method
  produced a number, in public, on the record.
- p. 542 (the forecasting refusal — the Founding Letter's thesis, twenty-one years early): "I
  have never specialized in economic forecasting or market forecasting either. My own business
  has been largely based on the principle that if you can make your results independent of any
  views as to the future you are that much better off."
- p. 539 (on the hearings themselves): "If the study actually does put the market down, then the
  market was due to go down and should go down."
- p. 544 (THE mystery exchange, Fulbright asking how a 10 becomes a 30): "That is one of the
  mysteries of our business, and it is a mystery to me as well as to everybody else. We know from
  experience that eventually the market catches up with value. It realizes it in one way or
  another." Same page, on his firm's holdings: "we have no interest in endeavoring to persuade
  people to buy stocks in which we have an ownership. We have never done it and we never will do
  it." CAUTION: the oft-repeated "Fulbright asked how the market can trade above book value"
  framing is a paraphrase, not in the transcript; the nearest real exchange is pp. 525-526,
  Graham-Newman's own shares quoted above asset value ($1,100 NAV, ~$1,250-1,300 OTC).
- p. 519 (margin): "speculation on margin is expensive to the public, and is sound only when it
  is practiced by people who have a great deal of experience and a great deal of ability."
- p. 547 (margin, prepared statement): "On balance, I am inclined to favor strict controls for
  margin trading and a fairly rapid advance to the 100-percent-margin limit — that is, no
  borrowing at all — as the Federal Reserve becomes increasingly concerned about the extent of
  speculation. My reason is that it is basically unsound for nonprofessionals to borrow money to
  speculate in stocks — or in anything else."
- pp. 537-538 (participation): staff director Wallace cites 6½-7½ million stockholders; asked
  whether they realize insiders may know more, Graham: "the average experienced person would
  assume that some people are bound to know more about the company than he would, and possibly
  trade on the additional knowledge."
- p. 543 (the commodity-reserve currency plan — his inflation answer), and p. 548 (closing):
  "while Congress should not ordinarily meddle with the stock market, there are times when it
  deserves attention from Congress. We are probably in such a time at present."

## Verified context (the season)

- Fed margin requirements: 50%→60% effective January 4, 1955; 60%→70% effective April 23, 1955
  (Federal Reserve Annual Report, Table 8). The Fulbright committee's May 1955 report scolded the
  Fed that the two 10-point boosts were too little, too slow ("unhealthy speculative
  developments"); four Republicans dissented (TIME, "The Friendly Findings").
- The Dow first closed above its September 3, 1929 peak (381.17) on November 23, 1954, at 382.74
  — a twenty-five-year round trip.
- Shareownership: ~6.5 million individual shareowners in 1952 (Brookings census for the NYSE) →
  ~8.6 million by the NYSE's 1956 census — one-third growth in four years.
- Inflation, the sharpest nuance for the note: 1955 CPI was flat to slightly NEGATIVE (annual
  average ~−0.4%). The inflation questions Graham fielded were fears about the FUTURE — easy
  money, deficits, wage rounds — asked during actual price stability. The 2026 parallel is asked
  the same way, and the note should present that symmetry without deciding it.
