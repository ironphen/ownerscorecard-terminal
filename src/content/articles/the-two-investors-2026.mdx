In August of 1976, a fund executive named John Bogle brought the first retail index fund to market. The underwriters had hoped to raise $150 million. They raised $11.3 million, a 93 percent shortfall Bogle himself called an abject failure. Critics called the product un-American and a sure path to mediocrity. Fidelity's Edward Johnson said he could not believe the great mass of investors would be satisfied with receiving just average returns.

Ten thousand dollars placed in that failure at the end of 1976 grew to nearly $2.0 million by March 2026, dividends reinvested, net of fees. The judgment was loud and the arithmetic was quiet.

## The record of the calls

The prosecution has never rested.

In August 2016, the brokerage Sanford C. Bernstein published a note titled "The Silent Road to Serfdom: Why Passive Investing Is Worse Than Marxism," arguing that "a supposedly capitalist economy where the only investment is passive is worse than either a centrally planned economy or an economy with active market led capital management." The nine calendar years that followed returned roughly 252 percent, which answers a question Bernstein had not asked; his actual charge, that passive flows corrupt the market's pricing, remains ungraded to this day. In July 2017, Elliott Management wrote that "passive investing is in danger of devouring capitalism." In September 2019, Michael Burry told Bloomberg that index funds resembled "the bubble in synthetic asset-backed CDOs before the Great Financial Crisis," in that prices were no longer set by security-level analysis. Within six months a real crash arrived, and it was not a passive-liquidity event: the funds functioned, redemption met no failure of mechanism, and the index finished 2020 higher. A later public timing call of Burry's was the single word "Sell," tweeted in January 2023; eight weeks later he tweeted "I was wrong to say sell." In January 2021, Jeremy Grantham declared "a full-fledged, epic bubble" that would rank "right along with the South Sea bubble, 1929, and 2000"; the following year did deliver an 18 percent loss, and the five years from his call still compounded to roughly double what his readers began with. In February 2024, David Einhorn said "I view the markets as fundamentally broken." The two years that followed returned roughly 47 percent.

Two entries complicate the docket. GMO's forecast of January 2000, published weeks from the top, projected that the index would lose about 1.9 percent a year in real terms over the coming decade. It lost 3.5. That call was right, and it was a valuation call; the vehicle holding the asset never entered into it. The same firm's end-2010 forecast projected 0.4 percent real for the following seven years; the index delivered roughly 13.6 percent a year before inflation, a miss of more than ten points a year on any accounting of it. The price arithmetic has sometimes proved right. No case against the vehicle itself has yet done so, and even the best of the arithmetic once missed by the width of an entire bull market.

Meanwhile the evidence that produced indexing in the first place has not moved. As of mid-2025, 91 percent of actively managed large-cap funds had underperformed the S&P 500 over the trailing twenty years, and 86 percent over ten. In calendar 2025 alone, 79 percent underperformed, the fourth-worst year in the scorecard's history. Of the domestic equity funds that placed in the top quartile in 2021, 0.46 percent were still there through 2025. The fee is collected in every fund in every year. The skill, on twenty years of evidence, shows up in fewer than one fund in ten.

## The record of the thing

What the callers point at today is concentration, and the concentration is real.

The ten largest companies in the index reached roughly 41 percent of its value at the end of 2025, the highest weight on record; at the 2000 peak the figure was about 27 percent, and in 1980 it was about 26 percent, with seven of those ten in the oil and gas business. Measured on the holdings of the index funds themselves in early July 2026, the ten largest slots weigh about 37 percent, the largest names having fallen harder than their index this year. The ten are [Nvidia](/c/NVDA), [Apple](/c/AAPL), [Microsoft](/c/MSFT), [Amazon](/c/AMZN), [Alphabet](/c/GOOGL), [Broadcom](/c/AVGO), [Meta](/c/META), [Tesla](/c/TSLA), and [Micron](/c/MU), with the tenth seat belonging to Lilly or to Berkshire, depending on the day's prices.

The difference between this concentration and the last one is on the earnings line. At the March 2000 peak, the ten largest were 26.6 percent of the index's value and about 15.8 percent of its trailing earnings, a gap of eleven points. In March 2026 they were 37.4 percent of the value and 33.2 percent of the earnings, a gap of four. Today's giants carry a premium; 2000's carried a fantasy. The premium is still a premium: at the March reading the ten traded at 22 times forward earnings against just under 18 for the remaining 490. And seven of the ten, Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom, Meta, and Micron, sell the chips, the models, or the computing they run on; on the July fund holdings, that one wager weighs about 28 percent of the index.

Then there is the churn, which is the record's rebuttal to the permanence everyone assumes. Of 1980's top ten, two remained by 2000. Of 2000's top ten, exactly one, Microsoft, is in the top ten today; only eight of the ten still exist as companies; three lost money for their holders over the following twenty-four years, and exactly one outperformed the index it once led. The index fires its own leadership on a schedule no committee sets, and the investor who owned it owned every replacement without lifting a finger.

These are, mostly, extraordinary businesses. That was also true of the best of 2000's ten. The question a price asks is how long the extraordinary part lasts, and that question does not appear anywhere in the table.

Tokyo's index made its high in December 1989 and did not close above it again for thirty-four years; with dividends reinvested the round trip still took thirty-one. Milan's benchmark saw its first new record in twenty-six years in May 2026. Athens peaked in 1999 and remains roughly 60 percent below that peak a generation later. The American version was smaller but real: from the March 2000 peak the index lost half its value, recovered to breakeven in 2007, then lost 57 percent, and the full decade of 2000 through 2009 returned less than nothing. An index bought once, at the wrong top, has gone underwater for longer than a working life's patience.

But almost nobody buys an index once, at a top, with everything. The retirement investor buys it every two weeks for forty years, and that mechanical fact changes the arithmetic more than any forecast ever has. The cases below are computed from the monthly index data, dividends reinvested and nothing else. A contributor putting $500 a month into the index through the lost decade put in $60,000 and finished with $63,923; the same money as a lump sum at the start finished at $55,852. A contributor who began at the worst starting month an American investor has ever drawn, September 1929, and simply continued for twenty-five years compounded at about 12 percent a year through an 89 percent drawdown. Even in Japan, a steady contributor from January 1990 through the 2024 recovery earned about 5 percent a year on prices alone, dividends excluded, while the lump sum earned 5 percent in total across thirty-four years. The catastrophe stories are true, and they are stories about a date. What the contributor owns is a procedure. The procedure carried the American worst cases and the Japanese one. Athens it has not been shown to redeem.

## The blessing

The index needs no advocate here. The strongest case for it was made by the men this publication reads, in their own words and with their own money.

Graham's 1976 interview, given in the last months of his life, is remembered as a deathbed conversion to passivity, and the memory is imprecise in both directions. He did say he was "no longer an advocate of elaborate techniques of security analysis in order to find superior value opportunities," and he did argue that the average institutional manager cannot beat the market, calling the proposition that he could a logical contradiction. But he did not tell the individual investor to buy an index fund; he prescribed "a highly simplified" approach applying "a single criteria, or perhaps two criteria," to the price paid. The folklore flattens a precise man. What can be said exactly is that the index fund executes the defensive investor's two stated aims, safety by procedure and freedom from effort, by construction, and that the two papers that preceded it, Samuelson's challenge in 1974 and Ellis's "The Loser's Game" in 1975, are Graham's institutional arithmetic carried to its conclusion.

Buffett removed all doubt about where he stands. His will directs that 90 percent of a family bequest go into a very low-cost S&P 500 fund. He wagered publicly that an unmanaged index fund would beat five funds of hedge funds over ten years, and won without suspense: 125.8 percent against 87.7 for the best challenger and 2.8 for the worst. And in 1993 he wrote: "By periodically investing in an index fund, for example, the know-nothing investor can actually out-perform most investment professionals." Everyone quotes that. Buffett continued: the know-something investor should instead concentrate in the handful of businesses he actually understands. Munger put the same idea without the courtesy: the wise ones bet heavily when the world offers them the odds, and the rest of the time they do not bet at all.

## The sorting

Graham set the choice ahead of everything else in the book. "Investment policy, as it has been developed here, depends in the first place on a choice by the investor of either the defensive (passive) or aggressive (enterprising) role," he wrote, and the aggressive investor "must have a considerable knowledge of security values," enough to warrant viewing his operations as equivalent to a business enterprise. Then the sentence the half century since has done nothing to soften: "There is no room in this philosophy for a middle ground, or a series of gradations, between the passive and aggressive status. Many, perhaps most, investors seek to place themselves in such an intermediate category; in our opinion that is a compromise that is more likely to produce disappointment than achievement."

The defensive investor's aims are safety and freedom from effort, and everything Graham prescribes for him, and everything Bogle later built for him, follows from those two aims. The enterprising investor is marked by the willingness to devote time and care to selection, and by the quieter condition Graham attached: ability enough that the operations amount to a business. He put a number on the threshold, too. The work is not worth undertaking unless the investor could hope to add, say, five percentage points a year before taxes on the stock portion of what he owns. Graham wrote hope, not expect.

Graham's dabbler, the man in the intermediate category, had to telephone a broker to act on his compromise. Today's carries the temptation in his pocket, commission-free, and no push notification ever arrives to say that sitting still was good for the account.

Set against Graham's choice, the whole argument about indexing changes shape. Whether the index is too concentrated, whether the multiple is too high, whether the coming decade's average will be thin: these are consequential questions for the defensive investor, and the record on them is above. For the enterprising investor they are close to idle. His result comes out of the spread between the average and what continuous, careful selection can find, and Graham's five points are earned in that spread or nowhere. The average can be generous and the work still worthless. The average can be nothing for a decade and the work worth everything.

There is a mechanical fact about how the two investors now share a market, and it is Bernstein's complaint read from the other side. Every dollar the defensive investor adds buys the list in proportion to what already leads it, by construction and without a judgment; whatever is small, spun off, deleted from the list, or simply odd receives none of that marginal dollar, also by construction and also without a judgment. Bernstein argued in 2016 that this corrupts the market's pricing. Whether it does, at the market's scale, the record has not graded. Munger, asked at the 2022 Berkshire meeting about the index funds' power over corporate governance, granted the limit and denied it was here in the same breath: "I think indexing, if it gets to 90%, then it won't work very well at all. But at the moment, it's worked fine."

The sorting demands honesty about which investor one is, because the cost of confusion runs in both directions. The defensive investor who believes he is enterprising pays the five points instead of earning them, and the longer the horizon, the harsher his aggregate grade. The enterprising investor who believes the index question is his question spends his working hours on the one variable his work cannot move.

## The arithmetic, today

What can be said about the present, without a forecast, is the price.

As of July 2, the index trades at 27.8 times its trailing earnings and 20.4 times the coming year's estimates, both above their five- and ten-year averages. The Shiller multiple stands at 41.6 against a record of 44.2, set in December 1999. The dividend yield, at 1.05 percent, is the lowest ever recorded. Against a 10-year Treasury at 4.49 percent, the index's trailing earnings yield of 3.6 percent sits 0.9 points below the bond, though on forward estimates, which embed earnings growth above twenty percent for a second straight quarter and the largest upward revisions since 2021, the yield edges back above it. A near-record multiple sits on top of record and rapidly rising earnings.

The forecasting houses have published their numbers. Vanguard's capital markets model projects 2.8 to 4.8 percent a year, in nominal terms, for U.S. equities over the coming decade; Goldman Sachs projected a nominal 3 percent a year for the ten years from late 2024; AQR estimates 3.9 percent real; GMO's current seven-year forecast for large-cap America is negative in real terms. Beside those numbers belongs the docket above: the same GMO shop right in 2000, and wrong by more than ten points a year in 2010. If the thin decade arrives, the defensive procedure has survived thin decades before, on the 1929 and 2000 arithmetic. And if it arrives, the spread between the average and the carefully chosen is where anything above thin will have to come from.
