The companies that make video games describe their industry the way the companies that make movies describe theirs. It is a hit business, and the defining risk is the flop. Capcom, of Osaka, says as much in its integrated report, which lists "dependence on popular series and shortage of hit titles" among the major risks of its market. Then it reports the numbers, and the numbers have the wrong shape for a hit business: operating income has risen every year for thirteen years by the company's count, ten of them verifiable filing by filing, and for eleven consecutive years the increase has exceeded ten percent.

A record like that, in a market like that, is either the product of a durable machine or a long run of weather. The filings say a good deal about which is which. They also say what the machine now needs: more capital than it has ever consumed before.

## The record

The decade, from the company's results filings, in billions of yen:

| FY ended March | Net sales | Operating income | Margin |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2016 | 77.0 | 12.0 | 15.6% |
| 2018 | 94.5 | 16.0 | 17.0% |
| 2020 | 81.6 | 22.8 | 28.0% |
| 2022 | 110.1 | 42.9 | 39.0% |
| 2024 | 152.4 | 57.1 | 37.5% |
| 2026 | 195.4 | 75.3 | 38.5% |

Operating income rose in the omitted years as well; sales did not rise in every year of the decade. In the year ended March 2020 sales fell eighteen percent, and operating income rose anyway. The streak is not a sales streak; it was made by a change in what a sale is. In the year ended March 2022, three-quarters of the copies Capcom sold were digital downloads; in the year just ended, ninety-three percent were. A download carries no disc and no freight, and the climb from fifteen percent to thirty-eight followed that shift. The core game business runs richer still: the Digital Contents segment, about three-quarters of sales, earned a forty-nine percent segment margin last year, and its profit came to roughly ninety-four percent of the company's operating income. The rest of the company, arcades and amusement machines, is small. The ten years from March 2017 through March 2026, every line traced to the filing it came from, are on the [company's page](/jp/9697).

The balance sheet is spare. No bank borrowings, an equity ratio of 78.8 percent, ¥148 billion of cash and deposits, and ¥15 billion of investment securities bought during the year. Return on equity was 22.1 percent, above twenty in each of the five years the securities report tabulates.

Two cautions belong beside the table. First, the yen. Nearly nine in ten copies are sold overseas at prices set in dollars, and the company discloses a sensitivity of roughly ¥400 to 500 million of operating income for each one-yen move against its planning rate. Capcom publishes no constant-currency figure, so the reader cannot cleanly separate the operating gain from the translation gain; the latest plan assumes 140 yen to the dollar, against about 105 in the plan of May 2021. Second, the forecasts. In each of the past five years the first May forecast was beaten by between 1.9 and 5.9 percent. The stated mid-term target is the streak itself, ten percent operating-income growth every year, and next year's guidance is set at plus 10.2 percent, exactly on it.

## The catalog

A game, once built, costs nearly nothing to sell again. Capcom's accounting states the consequence: development cost is capitalized as work in progress in inventory and charged against sales as the copies sell, so a title that keeps selling after its cost is fully amortized delivers revenue that is almost entirely margin. Last year the company sold 59.1 million copies, a record, and, by the results presentation's count, 83.7 percent of them were catalog titles.

The catalog exists because the properties are owned and old. One hundred twenty-seven Capcom titles have each sold more than a million copies. Resident Evil has sold roughly 201 million units across its series; Monster Hunter, 127 million. The company works this shelf with a pricing ladder it sets out in its integrated report: a major title launches at sixty to seventy dollars, then steps down over roughly five years toward ten dollars and five, with the cuts timed to new-title announcements so that each release lifts the back catalog behind it. Development cost is typically recovered in the first year or two; the ladder is where the profit lives.

The remakes are the same idea at full price. The remake of Resident Evil 2 has sold 18.3 million copies, more than any other title in the franchise's history, and the remakes of Resident Evil 3 and 4 have sold 13.3 and 13.6 million. The assets and the audience already existed; the company rebuilt what it owned and sold it again. Underneath sits the RE Engine, Capcom's proprietary toolset, which the company presents as making each game cheaper to build than the last: by its description, assets are "shared across titles" and "rendering to editing, playtesting, and quality control can be completed within RE ENGINE."

The caution is the company's own. Capcom names "declining IP value due to brand saturation in long-running series" among its major risks, and management wrote in last year's integrated report: "We consistently release two to three major new titles each year, but recognize the need to grow our pipeline going forward." Capcom discloses no profit by franchise, but the top of its million-seller list is nearly all Monster Hunter and Resident Evil, and the durable catalog is mostly those two names. Monster Hunter Wilds, the flagship released at the end of February 2025, passed ten million copies within a month, the fastest any Capcom title had reached that figure by the company's announcement, then added almost nothing: 11.0 million by December, 11.4 million by March, against 22.1 million for Monster Hunter: World and 18.6 million for Rise. Resident Evil Requiem, released in February, sold 6.91 million copies by the end of March. Two franchises carry most of the shelf, and the newest flagship of one of them fell well short of its two predecessors.

Pragmata, an all-new property and the first answer to that pipeline sentence, shipped in April, the first month of the new fiscal year, and sold a million copies in two days and two million in sixteen, by the company's announcements; it was, in Capcom's words, "developed primarily by a team of younger Capcom developers," its cost was "comparatively lower than those of our major IPs," and investment in new properties "is not determined by the sales performance of individual titles." Onimusha: Way of the Sword, the first new title in that series in more than twenty years, arrives September 4; the company calls the project "re-activating IPs that haven't had a new title launch recently." Resident Evil Veronica, a remake of Code: Veronica, and a large Monster Hunter Wilds expansion are announced for 2027. The plan for the year underway calls for sixty-five million units, twelve million of them from new titles. The new names do not yet carry weight: by the company's disclosure, the Resident Evil series alone accounted for more than half of all units sold last year.

## The capital

For most of the streak, what the filings showed about Capcom's capital was how little the business consumed. Development investment, the company's measure of what it puts into making games, sat between ¥25 and ¥30 billion a year for seven years through fiscal 2022, while operating income tripled. From March 2017 to March 2026 the company retained about ¥190 billion of additional equity and added ¥62 billion of annual operating income, roughly thirty-two yen of new pre-tax operating income for every hundred retained. Most of the retained capital never entered the business at all; it accumulated as cash, time deposits, and now securities. Measured against the capital the business actually employed, the incremental return runs above one hundred percent pre-tax. That is our arithmetic, not the company's, and it is crude: endpoints nine years apart, pre-tax operating income against book equity, two tender buybacks ignored.

Buffett set out the standard for reading such a record in his 1992 letter: "Leaving the question of price aside, the best business to own is one that over an extended period can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return. The worst business to own is one that must, or will, do the opposite - that is, consistently employ ever-greater amounts of capital at very low rates of return." And in the same paragraph: "Unfortunately, the first type of business is very hard to find: Most high-return businesses need relatively little capital. Shareholders of such a business usually will benefit if it pays out most of its earnings in dividends or makes significant stock repurchases." His enduring example of the high-return business that needs little capital, set down in his 2007 letter, was See's Candies, which earned sixty percent pre-tax on $8 million of capital at purchase, and over the following thirty-five years required only $32 million of reinvestment while sending virtually all of its $1.35 billion of cumulative pre-tax earnings to its owner. Which of the two kinds Capcom's decade describes is the question the arithmetic above lets an owner ask.

Management is now testing whether the machine can employ far more capital than it ever has. Development investment broke its seven-year plateau and has stepped up about fourteen percent a year: ¥37.7 billion, ¥43.0, ¥48.9, ¥54.8, with ¥62.9 billion planned for the current year. Work in progress on game software has more than tripled from its 2019 trough, to ¥54.6 billion. A third research and development building is rising beside the Osaka head office, due in 2027, and another ¥10 billion of land nearby has been bought for the base after that. The company has hired more than a hundred developers every year since 2013, by its own account, and plans to keep doing so; development staff now number just over three thousand, three-quarters of all employees. This year a new line appeared on the balance sheet: ¥9.9 billion of media assets, the company's co-investment in a Street Fighter picture due in October. The stated ambition all this serves is one hundred million copies a year, against fifty-nine million just achieved.

The company describes its discipline for the new spending: a rolling five-to-ten-year title map on which every project is assigned its investment first, then a profit plan and a return on that investment, with formal approvals gating each stage from project start through prototype to production. Asked at the third-quarter briefing about the swelling work-in-progress balance, management answered, in the briefing's official English summary, that "we manage ROI on a title-by-title basis to ensure investment levels remain appropriate relative to planned sales volumes." Whether the discipline holds at the new scale is what the next several years of filings will show, because the company's disclosure says development costs are on a rising trend as the hardware advances.

Then there is the cash that was never deployed: ¥163 billion of cash, deposits, and the securities bought this year, with no bank borrowings against it. The stated policy for net cash, from the May briefing's official English summary, is "to secure a level that allows us to maintain our current level of development investment for a certain period." The period is not quantified. Three years of development investment at last year's level would be about ¥164 billion, roughly where the pile sits; the three-year yardstick is this note's illustration. The flagship titles take two years or more to build, and the development investment the cash is held against is rising. Returns to owners follow a stated thirty-percent payout guideline, with the filed ratio at 34.5 percent in each of the past two years. Dividends per share, adjusted for two stock splits, rose from ¥17.75 to ¥45 over the five years to March 2026, and there have been two tender buybacks in a decade: ¥6.0 billion in 2018 and ¥13.6 billion in 2022. Buffett gave the test for retained earnings its arithmetic form in 1984: "Unrestricted earnings should be retained only when there is a reasonable prospect - backed preferably by historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful analysis of the future - that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners." Whether Capcom's retained yen have done that is a question of market value, and this note consults no market price.

Stewardship is concentrated. Kenzo Tsujimoto, the company's founder, is chairman and chief executive; his son Haruhiro is president and chief operating officer; identifiable family holdings, the asset company Crossroad and three family members listed among the largest shareholders, came to 18.69 percent of the shares outstanding after treasury stock as of March 31, 2026, and stakes below the table's disclosure threshold, including any held by the founder personally, do not show. Half the board's fourteen directors are outside directors, all filed as independent, and the company holds no listed cross-shareholdings, which is rare among Japanese companies.

The capital record leaves an owner two questions. Is a cash reserve the size of three years of development investment the right one for a company whose inventory is future games, or more than it needs? And will the enlarged investment earn what the old investment earned? On the first, the reader has the policy and the arithmetic. On the second, the company has its gates and its record, and a development cost curve it says is rising.

## The machines

On Steam, 7,818 titles had disclosed the use of generative artificial intelligence in their making as of July of last year, about one in five of that year's new releases to that point. The disclosures are voluntary, so the count is a floor.

Capcom has stated its position in two halves. To investors in February: no generative-AI-made assets in its game content, and active use of the technology for efficiency and productivity in development, with uses under study in graphics, sound, and programming. And in practice, at scale: Google Cloud's April press release describes Capcom's testing agents "operating for more than 30,000 hours per month," watching game screens and predicting where systems will break; another agent preserves veteran engineers' answers in searchable form. Shinichi Inoue, the vice president in charge of the company's development platform and AI solutions, put the dividing line this way at Google's conference in April: "Even AI with the highest level of intelligence still does not match our creators when it comes to sensibility." (The quotation is our translation of remarks delivered in Japanese.) None of it in the games; all of it in the making of them.

Whether that line protects the advantages described above is an open question, and the evidence is divided. A Morgan Stanley research note in April, as quoted by Reuters, estimated that such tools could reduce development costs by nearly half industry-wide and saw "value concentrating in scaled platforms and discovery, particularly among companies with proprietary data, IP, and live operations"; by accounts of the same note, it also saw more competition in mid-scale games as the cost of making them falls. The case that the machines widen Capcom's advantage: development cost is large and rising, and the company names it as a risk, so a proprietary engine plus automated testing compounds a cost edge a small studio cannot replicate, while the volume of new releases makes the shelf harder to find and a trusted name more valuable on it. Resident Evil Requiem's 6.91 million copies by the end of March were sold into a market in which Steam alone had been adding roughly 350 new titles a week. The case that they erode it is written in Capcom's own risk box: "concerns about copyright infringement and damage to brand value due to the spread of AI," and, of the industry entire, "as barriers to entry fall, the stability of the industry as a whole may be shaken." Owned franchises and a trusted name, whatever moat they amount to, have never faced a competitor whose marginal game costs almost nothing to make.

The record shows a hit business converted into a catalog business, earning returns that our crude ruler puts above one hundred percent pre-tax on the capital it actually employed. The decision now underway is to employ more capital than the business has ever consumed, in an industry whose stability, the company's risk box says, may be shaken as barriers to entry fall. An owner leaves with the questions the record poses: the size of the reserve, and whether the enlarged investment earns what the old one did. The fourteenth year is guided at ten percent.
