In November 2021, Adobe traded just under $700. In the summer of 2026, near $220. In between, the company did not shrink. It grew.

<DeratingChart chart="price" />

Revenue per share rose from about $33 to about $56. Earnings per share rose from $10.02 to $16.70, up two-thirds. The share count fell, from 481 million to 427 million, bought down with tens of billions in repurchases. Operating margin held where it started, in the mid-thirties. By every line an owner reads, the business is larger and more profitable than it was at the top.

The price fell by two-thirds anyway. Every dollar of the decline came from one place, and it was not the earnings. It was the number the market would pay for them. Adobe went from roughly seventy times the prior year's earnings to roughly thirteen. The business did not de-rate. The market's confidence did.

Earnings up, price down, the whole difference in the multiple. That gap is the clearest case in years of something every investor should learn to spot.

## The record

Start where this site always starts: the business, in its own numbers, with no price attached. There is no price on Adobe's scorecard, [here](/c/ADBE), by design. The market's mood is not a fact about the company.

Ten years ago Adobe did $5.85 billion in revenue and earned $2.32 a share. Last year it did $23.8 billion and earned $16.70. Gross margin has sat near 88% the whole way. That is the economics of software, where the second copy costs nothing. Operating margin ran from 25% to a recent 37%. Return on the capital the business actually employs is in the low thirties. Diluted shares fell from 504 million to 427 million; since 2016 the company has spent roughly $45.7 billion retiring about 97 million of them.

Narrow the window to the one that matters, from the November 2021 peak to now. Over that stretch: revenue per share up about 70%, earnings per share up 67%, margins intact, share count down another 11%. There is no operating disappointment in this record to explain a two-thirds fall in the price. The business kept its side of the bargain.

So the fall came from somewhere else. To see where, you have to understand what a multiple actually is.

## The mechanism

Price is earnings times a multiple. Everyone knows the identity; almost no one reads the second term honestly. A price-to-earnings multiple is not a mood, and it is not a verdict on this year. **It is a claim about duration: how many years of earnings still lie ahead, and how sure the market is they will arrive.**

Most of the value in a growth company lives far out. Discount a stream of rising cash flows and the bulk of the present value sits past year ten, in the long tail nobody can see. A high multiple is the market saying it believes in that tail.

Part of Adobe's seventy, though, was never about Adobe. In late 2021 money was nearly free, and free money makes distant earnings look almost as good as present ones. So the whole growth-software shelf wore multiples it could not have worn at any other price of money. Then money stopped being free, and those multiples came in together, Adobe's with the rest. That leg of the fall is arithmetic, not judgment; it says nothing about this company in particular. Strip it out, and what is left is Adobe's alone. It is the horizon. When the multiple keeps compressing after the earnings have risen, and after rates have already done their work, the market is not saying this year was bad. It is saying it no longer knows how long the good years last.

<DeratingChart chart="pe" />

That is what the tape has been saying. Adobe has kept beating its numbers. It did more than $6.6 billion in a recent quarter, and its AI-first subscriptions reportedly passed $500 million, tripling in a year. The price has not followed. A business whose earnings rise while its stock does not is not being punished for its earnings. It is being repriced for its horizon. The market has not marked down the present. It has stopped underwriting the future.

The question, then, is whether it is right to.

## Adobe, in its own words

The way to argue both sides of that question is to let the company make each case in its own filing, under the signatures of people who can be sued for lying. Adobe's most recent annual report makes both.

Here is the toll-bridge case, in the company's own words. Firefly, its generative engine, is "commercially safe... trained on data Adobe has the rights to use," every output carries a Content Credentials "nutrition label," and enterprise customers "are eligible to obtain an intellectual property indemnification for AI content." That is not marketing. It is the exact thing a large company's legal department needs before it can put generative art into a product it ships. If the moat is real, that is where it lives. Not in the pixels, which anyone's model can now make. In the indemnity, the audit trail, the permission to use the work without being sued.

Here is the melting-ice-cube case, from the same document, this time from the risk factors, where a company has to name what could go wrong. Its AI innovations may not "be successful, adopted or monetizable"; "consumer reception and monetization pathways are uncertain"; it is investing in AI "without assurance that our customers and users will adopt them," and warns the cost "could adversely impact our margins." Adobe now lists "AI inferencing costs" by name as part of what it costs to deliver its own software. The disruption has entered the income statement.

Two facts sharpen the tension past the boilerplate. First, the slowdown is real, and it is partly hidden. Adobe guided next year's recurring revenue to grow about 10%. But roughly $480 million of that comes from an acquisition; strip it out and the organic rate is closer to 8%. Second, and cutting the other way, Adobe has done the one thing a toll bridge does when a faster road opens: it charged the road a toll. Firefly is now a *multi-model* product. Adobe sells access to its rivals' engines, Google's and OpenAI's among them, *inside its own workflow*, and meters them by the credit. It turned the disruptors into suppliers.

Which of those is the truer picture of 2030? The filing does not say, because it cannot know. Neither can we. But we are not the first to stand in front of this question, and we can look at how it has gone before.

## Two histories

De-ratings end in two very different places, and the whole discipline is in refusing to assume which one you are in.

**Sometimes the market was wrong, and the fall was the opportunity.** Apple in 2013 traded near nine times earnings on a story that the iPhone had peaked and the magic died with its founder; it was a toll bridge, and the toll compounded for a decade. Meta in 2022 fell to nine times on "the metaverse is a bonfire and TikTok is eating them"; the business was never in the danger the price implied. In the larger frame, a new technology has killed some franchises and spared others, and the volume of the fear tells you nothing about which. The automated teller machine was supposed to end the bank teller. Instead the count of tellers roughly doubled between 1970 and 2010, because the machines made branches cheap enough that banks opened far more of them. Automating a task is not automating the job. A moat rooted in a real, distinct job to be done can absorb a new technology. Sometimes it swallows it.

**And sometimes the market was right, the moat was real, and it melted anyway.** Encyclopædia Britannica was the finest object in its category for two centuries. Einstein and Freud were among its contributors. It sold door to door to parents who wanted the best for their children, 120,000 sets a year at the 1990 peak, near $650 million in revenue. Then Microsoft pressed the same knowledge onto a disc that cost about a dollar-fifty to make and bundled it free with a new computer. Britannica's revenue fell by half in three years. Its moat did not narrow; the *format* it was built on became worthless. The best product in the world lost to a worse one that was free, because the thing people were buying had simply stopped being a thing they needed to buy.

The public version of that story, the one with a stock chart, is Kodak. It carries the warning that matters most here. Kodak held eighty percent of American film, invented the digital camera in its own lab in 1975, and then spent thirty years being killed by it. Note the timing, because that is the lesson: **the stock did not fall early. It fell late.** Kodak traded above $20 as recently as 2007, a decade after digital substitution was obvious and three years after digital cameras outsold film. Then it collapsed, to sixty-five cents and into bankruptcy by 2012. For ten years it looked statistically cheap. It looked cheap all the way down. A de-rating that has already happened is not proof the melting is finished. Kodak's holders learned that a low multiple on a dying franchise is not a floor. It is a slope.

Even the vindications took a punishing time. Cisco was the most valuable company on earth in 2000, at two hundred times earnings. The business went on to roughly quintuple, and the stock spent the next quarter-century below that peak. The buyer was not wrong about the company. He was wrong about the price, and the multiple took twenty-five years to forgive him. Microsoft tripled its earnings between 2000 and 2013, and its stock went nowhere the whole time. A lost decade of pure multiple compression from a bubble start. Then the business re-rated and was vindicated. The toll bridge held. It just made its owners wait thirteen years to find out.

Line those up and the shape is plain. Cisco: you can overpay for a great business and wait decades. Microsoft: a real moat can look dead for a decade and be right all along. Britannica: a real moat can simply die. Kodak: and when it dies, the market is usually *late*, so "already cheap" tells you nothing. Adobe sits somewhere inside that range. No number on its scorecard, or anyone's, tells you where.

## The peers

Adobe is not alone on the table. The whole software complex is being asked this question at once. Pull up Adobe's [peers](/c/ADBE#sec-peers) and the striking thing is not that they all de-rated. It is that the market de-rated them with a single brush while the record shows they are nothing alike.

Set them side by side on owner economics and the spread is enormous. Adobe earns a 33% return on the capital it employs and keeps 39 cents of owner earnings on the dollar. [Intuit](/c/INTU) is much the same, high returns and fat margins. [ServiceNow](/c/NOW) posts a thin GAAP operating margin but converts to owner earnings beautifully. [Salesforce](/c/CRM) runs a low-single-digit operating margin on enormous revenue. These are not one business wearing a single label. A fortress, a toll booth, a turnaround in progress, filed together because a screen calls them all "software."

Which is the point, and the discipline. "Software got cheap, buy the group" is exactly the lazy conclusion this site exists to refuse. The de-rating was sector-wide, but the answer to *melting or misunderstood* is name by name. You find it by reading each one, not by the label they share. Each of these will earn its own careful look here in time. None of them will get a rating.

## The pass

So here is where it lands.

You have scrolled this far looking for the line. Every other page you read about this stock has one. A target, a rating, a *buy the fear* or a *dead money*: the single word that lets you stop thinking. It is not here, and that is not an oversight. It is the whole product.

Adobe trades at thirteen times the earnings it just grew by two-thirds, and it carries more cash and investments than debt. I do not know whether that is Apple in 2013 or Kodak in 2007. Neither does anyone who tells you otherwise with confidence. The cases are both real and both filed. You can read the moat in the indemnity. You cannot read the horizon. The slowdown is real and partly masked; the toll-taking is real and maybe temporary. And the one pattern that applies most directly cuts against comfort. On the melts, the market is usually late. So the thing that looks most reassuring here, the low multiple already in hand, is the very thing that reassured Kodak's holders every year on the way down.

Charlie Munger kept three baskets: in, out, and too hard. The too-hard basket is not where you put bad companies. It is where an honest person puts most things, including cheap, great ones, when the deciding question is not one you can underwrite. Here that question is whether a two-century-old kind of moat survives a real technological turn. Warren Buffett got the discipline from Ted Williams, who carved the strike zone into seventy-seven cells and hit .400 in the center and .230 on the low outside corner, and whose whole method was to let the corner pitch go by *even though it was a strike*. Buffett's improvement is the part that frees you: in investing there are no called strikes. You do not have to swing. You can stand at the plate through a thousand pitches and lose nothing until you swing and miss.

Adobe at thirteen times, with a moat you cannot see the far side of, is a strike on the corner. This one goes in the too-hard pile. Not "avoid," which is a verdict. *Too hard*, which is a confession, a discipline, and, if the site is any good, a relief.

What would move it out? Not the price. What would make the pitch readable is what answers *melting or misunderstood*, and it arrives in the filings one quarter at a time: the organic recurring-revenue rate, with acquisitions stripped out, holding or breaking; Firefly's paid adoption, and whether the credits actually make money; whether pricing holds per seat or slides toward metered use as agents do the work seats used to; and, quietly against the reader, the fact that Adobe has already folded its reporting into one segment, taking away some of the granularity that would let you check. Watch those, and the corner pitch may drift into the center, or off the plate entirely. Until it does, the honest thing, and the hard thing, is to keep the bat on your shoulder.

The business grew. The price fell. Both are facts, and neither is the answer. The answer is that some questions are not yet knowable. The person who admits it keeps his capital, his judgment, and his reader's trust. In the end, that is the only record worth building.
