In November 2021, Adobe traded just under $700. In the summer of 2026, near $220. In between, the company did not shrink. It grew.

<DeratingChart chart="price" />

Revenue per share rose from about $33 to about $56. Earnings per share rose from $10.02 to $16.70, up two-thirds. The share count fell, from 481 million to 427 million, bought down with tens of billions in repurchases. Operating margin held where it started, in the mid-thirties. By every line an owner reads, the business is larger and more profitable than it was at the top.

The price fell by two-thirds anyway. Every dollar of the decline came out of the number the market would pay for the earnings: Adobe went from roughly seventy times the prior year's profit to roughly thirteen. The business did not de-rate. The market's confidence did.

That is a de-rating: a fall in price that is all multiple, with the earnings intact or rising. They rarely come this pure.

## The record

Start where this site always starts: the business, in its own numbers, with no price attached. There is no price on Adobe's scorecard, [here](/c/ADBE), by design; the market's mood is not a fact about the company. Suppose the exchange had closed in November 2021 and reopened this month. An owner reading only the filings would conclude his company had done splendidly, because it had. Everything that went wrong lives in the one number the scorecard leaves out.

Ten years ago Adobe did $5.85 billion in revenue and earned $2.32 a share. Last year it did $23.8 billion and earned $16.70. Gross margin has sat near 88% throughout; that is the economics of software, where the second copy costs nothing. Operating margin ran from 25% to a recent 37%. Return on the capital the business actually employs is in the low thirties. Diluted shares fell from 504 million to 427 million; since 2016 the company has spent roughly $45.7 billion retiring about 97 million of them.

Narrow the window to the one that matters, from the November 2021 peak to now. Over that stretch: revenue per share up about 70%, earnings per share up 67%, margins intact, share count down another 11%. Nothing in the operating record explains a two-thirds fall in the price. The business kept its side of the bargain.

So the fall came from somewhere else. To see where, you have to understand what a multiple actually is.

## The mechanism

Price is earnings times a multiple. The first term is audited; the second is the market's to give and to take away, and the second is where all of Adobe's decline lives. A multiple compounds two judgments: the price of money, and a judgment about duration, meaning how many years of earnings still lie ahead and how sure anyone can be that they arrive.

Duration has a plainer name. A moat is nothing but the power of a business to go on earning after competition arrives to take the earnings away, and what a multiple prices, once the price of money has taken its share, is that power. The moat and the multiple are one subject with two names. The moat is the claim, the multiple is its price, and the price is revised daily whether or not the claim has changed.

At seventy times, a buyer was handing over seventy dollars for every dollar of the previous year's profit, and much of what he paid was for years past 2030 that nobody, including Adobe's own managers, can describe. A multiple that high is faith that the moat has no visible end.

Part of the seventy, though, was never about Adobe. In late 2021 money was nearly free, and free money makes far-off earnings look nearly as good as present ones, so the whole growth-software shelf wore multiples it could not have worn at any other price of money. Then money stopped being free, and those multiples came in together, Adobe's with the rest. How much of the fall was the price of money and how much was doubt about the moat cannot be split exactly; no such instrument exists. But the shelf itself is a rough control. The group de-rated together when rates rose, and what a name lost beyond the group, after its earnings had risen and rates had done their work, is the market's answer on its moat alone. When that part keeps compressing, the market is saying it no longer knows how long the good years last.

<DeratingChart chart="pe" />

Adobe has kept beating its numbers. It did more than $6.6 billion in a recent quarter, and its AI-first subscriptions reportedly passed $500 million, tripling in a year. The price has not followed. The market has not marked down the present. It has stopped underwriting the future.

Graham's line, as Buffett tells it, is that the market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run. A de-rating this long and this deep is the market claiming its weighing is done. Whether it is, whether three years of falling multiple on rising earnings is the scale settling or a long count of votes, is the question. Nobody rings a bell when the voting stops.

## Adobe, in its own words

The fair way to hear both cases is to let Adobe argue them itself, in its most recent annual report, under the signatures of people who can be sued for lying. It argues both.

Here is the toll-bridge case. Firefly, its generative engine, is "commercially safe... trained on data Adobe has the rights to use"; every output carries a Content Credentials "nutrition label"; enterprise customers "are eligible to obtain an intellectual property indemnification for AI content." That is the assurance a big company's lawyers require before generative art can ship in a product. If the moat is real, it lives there: not in the pixels, which anyone's model can now make, but in the indemnity, the audit trail, the permission to use the work without being sued.

Here is the melting-ice-cube case, from the same document, in the risk factors, where a company must name what could go wrong. Its AI innovations may not "be successful, adopted or monetizable"; "consumer reception and monetization pathways are uncertain"; it is investing in AI "without assurance that our customers and users will adopt them," and it warns the cost "could adversely impact our margins." Adobe now lists "AI inferencing costs" by name among the costs of delivering its own software. The disruption has entered the income statement.

Two facts cut deeper than the boilerplate. First, the slowdown is real and partly hidden. Adobe's guidance calls for recurring revenue to grow about 10%, but roughly $480 million of that was bought, not grown; strip the acquisition out and the organic rate is closer to 8%. Second, and cutting the other way, Adobe has done the one thing a toll bridge can do when a faster road opens: charge the road. Firefly is now a multi-model product. Adobe sells access to its rivals' engines, Google's and OpenAI's among them, inside its own workflow, and meters them by the credit. It turned the disruptors into suppliers.

Which is the truer picture of 2030? The filing does not say, because it cannot know. Neither can we. But the question has been answered before, both ways.

## Two histories

De-ratings end in two very different places, and the discipline is in refusing to assume which one you are in.

Sometimes the market was wrong, and the fall was the opportunity. Apple in 2013 traded near nine times earnings on a story that the iPhone had peaked and the magic had died with its founder; the fear was wrong, and the earnings compounded for another decade. Meta in 2022 fell to nine times on "the metaverse is a bonfire and TikTok is eating them"; the business was never in the danger the price implied. A new technology has killed some franchises and spared others, and the volume of the fear tells you nothing about which. The automated teller machine was supposed to end the bank teller; instead the count of tellers roughly doubled between 1970 and 2010, because the machines made branches cheap enough that banks opened far more of them. Automating a task is not automating the job. A moat rooted in work people still need done can absorb a new technology. Sometimes it swallows it.

And sometimes the market was right, the moat was real, and it melted anyway. Encyclopædia Britannica was the finest object in its category for two centuries. Einstein and Freud were among its contributors. It sold door to door to parents who wanted the best for their children, 120,000 sets a year at the 1990 peak, near $650 million in revenue. Then Microsoft pressed a lesser encyclopedia onto a disc that cost about a dollar-fifty to make and bundled it free with a new computer. Britannica's revenue fell by half in three years. The moat had not narrowed; the format it guarded became worthless, and the best product in the world lost to a worse one that was free.

The public version of that story, the one with a stock chart, is Kodak. Kodak held eighty percent of American film, invented the digital camera in its own laboratory in 1975, and then spent thirty years being killed by it. The market trailed the melt the entire time: Kodak still traded above $20 in 2007, three years after digital cameras outsold film and more than thirty years after Kodak's own laboratory built the thing that was killing it; by 2012 it was at sixty-five cents, in bankruptcy. For years it screened statistically cheap. It looked cheap all the way down. Part of the reason a market runs late on a melt is not in the numbers. The holder who has once called the stock cheap is committed, every lower price makes his old argument sound better, and denial finishes what commitment starts. Kodak's holders learned that a low multiple on a dying franchise is not a floor. It is a slope.

Even the happy endings ran long. Cisco was the most valuable company on earth in 2000, at two hundred times earnings. The business went on to roughly quintuple, and the stock spent the next quarter-century below its peak; the buyer was not wrong about the company, he was wrong about the price, and the multiple took twenty-five years to forgive him. Microsoft tripled its earnings between 2000 and 2013 and its stock went nowhere, a lost decade of pure multiple compression from a bubble start. Then the business re-rated. The toll bridge held. It had merely made its owners wait thirteen years to find out.

In these histories the multiple ended up where the moat was; the only variable was how long the trip took, and it was rarely short. Buffett says time is the friend of the wonderful business and the enemy of the mediocre one. A multiple is the market's guess at which of the two it is holding, and a de-rating from seventy to thirteen is that guess moving from one column toward the other. Whether the guess is right is another matter. Adobe sits somewhere inside these histories, and no number on its scorecard, or anyone's, says where.

## The peers

Adobe is not alone on the table; the software complex is being asked the same question all at once. Pull up Adobe's [peers](/c/ADBE#sec-peers). They all de-rated, and the striking thing is that the market priced them as one thing while the record shows they are nothing alike.

Set them side by side on owner economics and the spread is enormous. Adobe earns a 33% return on the capital it employs and keeps 39 cents of owner earnings on the dollar. [Intuit](/c/INTU) is much the same, high returns and fat margins. [ServiceNow](/c/NOW) posts a thin GAAP operating margin but converts to owner earnings beautifully. [Salesforce](/c/CRM) runs a low-single-digit operating margin on enormous revenue. These are not one business wearing a single label. A fortress, a franchise, a turnaround in progress, filed together because a screen calls them all "software."

The table has a second use. The shelf de-rated together when money changed price, and every name paid that toll; what a name lost beyond the shelf is the part its own moat must answer for.

"Software got cheap, buy the group" is the lazy conclusion. The de-rating was sector-wide; the answer to *melting or misunderstood* comes name by name, from reading each one, not from the label they share. Each of these will earn its own careful look here in time. None of them will get a rating.

## The pass

You have scrolled this far looking for the line. Every other page on this stock has one: a target, a rating, a *buy the fear* or a *dead money*. It is not here, and that is not an oversight. It is the whole product.

Adobe trades at thirteen times the earnings it just grew by two-thirds, and it carries more cash and investments than debt, the kind of balance sheet that makes a long wait survivable. I do not know whether that is Apple in 2013 or Kodak in 2007. Neither does anyone who says otherwise with confidence; the verdict is what those pages are paid to produce, and nobody is paid for "too hard." The cases are both real and both filed. You can read the moat in the indemnity. The far side of it is written down nowhere. The slowdown is real and partly masked; the toll-taking is real and maybe temporary. And the melt on that list is a standing warning: Kodak's price trailed the facts for years, and the multiple that was already low is exactly what reassured its holders on the way down.

Graham's rule was the margin of safety: pay enough below a business's value that your errors cannot ruin you. But a margin is the difference between two numbers, and here one of the numbers is missing. What Adobe is worth turns almost entirely on how long the moat holds, and that is the question no one can answer. Where no value can be appraised, no margin can be computed; thirteen times, standing alone, is only a number. Graham drew the further line with a lawyer's care: an operation that cannot be carried through analysis to a conclusion is not an investment, whatever the buyer calls it. A purchase here is a speculation on the life of the moat. Nothing forbids it. Honesty only asks that it go by its right name.

Charlie Munger kept three baskets: in, out, and too hard. Most honest work ends in the third, cheap and great things included, whenever the deciding question cannot be answered at any price. Here the deciding question is whether a two-century-old kind of moat survives a real technological turn. Warren Buffett took the discipline from Ted Williams, who carved the strike zone into seventy-seven cells, knew he hit .400 in the middle and .230 at the low outside corner, and let the corner pitch go by even though it was a strike. Buffett's improvement on Williams is the part that frees you: in investing there are no called strikes. You can stand at the plate all season and lose nothing until you swing and miss.

Adobe at thirteen times, with a moat whose far side no one can see, is a strike on the corner. It goes in the too-hard pile. Not "avoid," which is a verdict. Too hard, which is a confession.

Before leaving it there, invert the question once. Ask what Adobe's record would look like today if the melt had already begun. It would look like this one. Britannica's revenue held until the disc shipped; Kodak screened cheap for a decade while the franchise died underneath. A presentable record is what the early years of a melt look like, which is why the record alone can neither clear the name nor convict it.

What would move it out of the pile? The price will not do it. Thirteen times is a fair price for a wonderful business and no bargain at all for a melting one, so no multiple can settle a question about quality. The answer arrives in the filings, one quarter at a time. Watch four things. First, the organic growth of recurring revenue, with bought revenue stripped out: holding, or breaking. Second, Firefly's paid adoption, and whether the credits actually make money. Third, whether pricing holds per seat or slides toward metered use as agents do the work seats used to do. Fourth, and running against you: Adobe has folded its reporting into a single segment, so some of the numbers you would want to check are no longer published. Until those answer, my bat stays on my shoulder.

The business grew. The price fell. Both are facts, and neither is the answer. Some questions cannot yet be answered. The investor who admits it keeps his capital.
